Listeners heard episode one (what the statements measure, book versus market, ratios as a map); episode three works through the exercises. Derive each result in words, state what it assumes, and say where it fails.

Why market values add up: the cash flow from assets is split exhaustively between creditors and owners, so the values of the two claims sum to the value of the assets. This additivity is the engine of Modigliani–Miller in Topic 2A. It survives any way of splitting the cash flows, unless the split changes the cash flows themselves, which is the moral-hazard case of Tirole Ch. 3 later in the course.

The DuPont identity: derive return on equity as profit margin times asset turnover times the equity multiplier, and pose the slides' question, "should managers maximize financial leverage?" Answer with Higgins §2.3's three problems with ROE (timing, risk, book value), then preview the formal answer: by MM II the required return on equity rises with leverage, so a higher ROE can come with no gain in value, the same logic as the bank-capital debate in Topic 2B. Present ROIC as a financing-neutral return and say where it still misleads, for example when investment is expensed rather than capitalised. Explain why growth, risk and leverage all move the P/E, and why EBITDA, interest coverage and economic earnings answer different questions.

Profits are not cash: walk through the three sections of the cash-flow statement and the sign of every operating adjustment. Depreciation is subtracted to compute taxable profit and then added back, because no cash leaves the firm; its only cash effect is the tax it saves, the tax rate times depreciation. A rise in receivables or inventory is cash tied up and is subtracted; a rise in payables is supplier credit and is added.

Build unlevered free cash flow as EBIT times one minus the tax rate, plus depreciation, minus capital expenditures, minus the change in net working capital. Capital expenditures are the cash paid for long-lived assets, net of the after-tax proceeds of assets sold, recorded when paid; the machine's cost is counted once there, which is why depreciation is not also treated as a cash cost. Explain why only the change in working capital enters (a constant level rolls over), and why working capital that is fully recovered at the end still destroys value: it comes back later. Derive the bridge from net income: add back after-tax interest, depreciation, then subtract capex and the change in working capital.

Free cash flow values the enterprise; free cash flow to equity, after after-tax interest and net borrowing, values the equity. Interest is excluded from the first because the tax benefit of debt is handled in the discount rate or the APV (Topic 2B). Then deferred taxes, PS1 Exercise 1(b): taxes on the income statement equal taxes paid plus the change in deferred taxes, so the increase in operating deferred tax liabilities is added back like depreciation, and the liability must not also be subtracted as debt.

The uses of free cash flow (creditors, reinvestment, cash reserves, dividends, buybacks) raise two open questions: does payout policy matter (dividend irrelevance, Topic 2B), and what do managers do with free cash, the agency and liquidity issues of Tirole Ch. 5? Tie in the rise in corporate cash (Faulkender et al., 2019) and its two explanations, precautionary saving or foreign taxes.
