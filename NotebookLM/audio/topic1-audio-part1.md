Explain and discuss at graduate depth (1/3) Topic 1 of Corporate Finance, the accounting review — concepts: what each financial statement measures, and where book numbers mislead. This is the first of three episodes: the second derives the ratio and cash-flow results, the third works through the exercises and the common misconceptions. Sources: `Aula_Topic1 - Accounting review.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Berk DeMarzo Harford - Fundamentals of Corporate Finance 2e (2011).pdf` (hereafter Berk, Ch. 2), `Livro_Jennifer L. Koski_ Robert C. Higgins_ Todd Mitton - Analysis for financial management (2022) - libgen.li.pdf` (hereafter Higgins, Ch. 1–2) and `Livro_Grinblatt Titman - Financial Markets and Corporate Strategy 2e (2002).pdf` (hereafter Grinblatt–Titman, Ch. 1–3). No arithmetic on air. Argue each claim, say when it fails, and name the later topic where it bites.

Firms sell goods and sell promises: a senior, fixed promise to creditors and a residual promise to owners. Present the balance sheet as the record of those promises, and contrast the instruments behind them with Grinblatt–Titman Ch. 2–3: seniority, maturity, covenants, limited liability, and why the residual claim carries the control rights. State that assets equal liabilities plus equity in market value as well as in the books, and flag that the next episode argues why.

Book versus market as an information problem. Recognition rules favour tangible assets, while intangibles (brands, know-how, customer relationships) can be most of enterprise value; historical cost is not market value. Use the Silicon Valley Bank case: why the split between held-to-maturity and available-for-sale securities hid losses that mattered for a bank funded with short-term deposits. Explain how Starbucks reached negative book equity with a market capitalisation above 120 billion dollars, why that is not insolvency, and what buybacks and accumulated losses do to book equity. Argue why market leverage and net debt measure leverage better, even though covenants are written on book numbers (Higgins §1.7, Berk §2.3).

The income statement as an allocation, not a cash record: accrual and matching, revenue recognised at the sale rather than the payment, and depreciation as the cost of a long-lived asset spread over its life. Walk from sales through gross profit and EBIT to net income, and say what each subtotal is for. Explain operating leverage: when most operating costs are fixed, a given fall in sales produces a larger fall in EBIT, so fixed-cost firms are hit hardest by demand shocks.

Liquidity and working capital. Define net working capital as operating current assets (receivables, inventory, the cash operations need) minus non-interest-bearing current liabilities (payables, accrued expenses), and say why short-term debt and excess cash are left out: they are financing, not operations. Explain the current and quick ratios, receivable, inventory and payable days, and why trade payables are a source of finance (the GPA balance sheet in the slides), which leads to the trade-credit topic later in the course.

Ratios as a map, each answering one question: profitability (gross, operating and net margins, ROA, ROE, ROIC), efficiency (asset turnover and the day counts), leverage (book and market debt-to-equity, net debt, interest coverage) and valuation (P/E, market-to-book, enterprise value multiples). Say which ratios mix operating and financing choices and which do not, and why the same ratio can mean different things in a bank, a retailer and a software firm. Leave the formal leverage argument for episode two.

Close by stating what episode two will derive: why market values add up, the DuPont decomposition and its limits, and the path from net income to free cash flow.
