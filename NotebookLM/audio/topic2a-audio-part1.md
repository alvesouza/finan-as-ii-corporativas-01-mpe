Explain and discuss at graduate depth (1/3) Topic 2A of Corporate Finance, valuation and the Modigliani–Miller benchmark — concepts: discount rates, cash flows, the NPV rule and the claims a firm issues. This is the first of three episodes: the second derives the unlevering formula, complete-markets pricing and the MM propositions, the third works through the exercises and misconceptions. Sources: `Aula_Topic2A - Valuation and MM.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Berk DeMarzo Harford - Fundamentals of Corporate Finance 2e (2011).pdf` (hereafter Berk, Ch. 8–9, 12–13), `Livro_Grinblatt Titman - Financial Markets and Corporate Strategy 2e (2002).pdf` (hereafter Grinblatt–Titman, Ch. 7, 11) and `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole). No arithmetic on air. State each idea precisely, the assumptions behind it, and what breaks when one fails. Listeners know Topic 1: market-value balance sheets and unlevered free cash flow.

Discount rates. Distinguish expected from realised returns, and spot rates from a single flat rate. Explain the risk premium as pay for systematic risk only: diversifiable risk is not priced because investors can remove it at no cost (Berk Ch. 12). Say what beta measures, why a project's discount rate is set by the risk of its cash flows and not by who finances it, and why the firm's cost of capital is the right rate only for projects as risky as the firm's average (divisional and project rates, Berk Ch. 13).

Cash flows. Only incremental cash flows count: include opportunity costs, such as an asset the firm already owns that could be sold, and exclude sunk costs, such as a consultant already paid, and allocated overhead that is incurred anyway. Explain why cash flows and discount rates must match: free cash flow to the firm with the WACC gives enterprise value; cash flow to equity with the cost of equity gives equity value. Mixing them is the most common valuation error.

The NPV rule and when it serves shareholders. Present NPV as the value created today for the owners, and state the conditions under which the project's NPV equals the gain to shareholders: symmetric information, competitive financiers who break even, and no risky debt already in place. Then break each one in words: with risky debt outstanding, part of a new project's value goes to creditors, so shareholders may reject a positive-NPV project (debt overhang, Topic 2B and Tirole Ch. 3); under asymmetric information, issuing equity to fund a good project can lower the share price (Tirole Ch. 6). Explain why the IRR is a shortcut that can rank projects wrongly or not exist.

Debt versus equity as contracts. Compare payoff, seniority, maturity, control rights, covenants and technical default, and limited liability. Describe the two payoff lines at maturity, debt flat at the face value once assets cover it and equity rising one-for-one above it, and say in words why equity is a call option on the firm's assets. Flag the conflict this creates when shareholders can choose risk (risk-shifting, Topic 2B).

Complete markets as an idea. A market is complete when every pattern of payoffs across future states can be built from traded securities. Explain the law of one price, state prices as the price today of one unit paid in one state only, and the risk-neutral view: discount expected payoffs at the risk-free rate under adjusted probabilities. Explain intuitively why a claim that pays in the bad state (the slump) costs more than its probability alone suggests.

Close by stating what episode two will derive: the asset beta as a value-weighted average, why no arbitrage means positive state prices, Fisher separation, and MM I and II.
