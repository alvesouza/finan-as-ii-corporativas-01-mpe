Discuss at graduate depth (2/3) the green-finance pick from episode one: `Artigo_Corporate Social Responsibility.pdf` (Hong and Shore, Annual Review of Financial Economics 2023), read by a listener who wants to know what green investing really buys and what it costs. Connect it to `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole, §1.8 "Shareholder Value or Stakeholder Society?" and §1.9) and to the WACC and tax-shield machinery in `Aula_Topic2B - Taxes, distress and trade-off.pdf`. No arithmetic on air. For every result, state the claim, the identification and where it can break.

Two views. The pecuniary view says CSR maximises long-run value because managers are short-termist or markets misprice climate and regulatory risk. The nonpecuniary view says investors give up returns to curb externalities, so brown stocks become the new sin stocks. Tie this to Bénabou and Tirole (2010), to Friedman's "other people's money" and to Tirole's case against a stakeholder mandate: many objectives blur accountability.

The equilibrium wedge. Walk through why stocks that are imperfect substitutes, combined with value-maximising firms that choose whether to qualify (Heinkel, Kraus and Zechner 2001), give r_S minus r_U equals minus m over q (Hong, Wang and Yang 2023). Explain why Tobin's q must be equal across types (firms are indifferent between qualifying and not), why the wedge is the dividend yield green investors sacrifice, and why the pecuniary view predicts a zero or positive gap instead. Note the CAPM version: negative alphas (Pástor, Stambaugh and Taylor 2021).

Tests 1 and 2, returns. Contrast realised returns with the implied cost of capital, and recall Merton's point that expected returns need long time series. Cover sin stocks beating comparables by about 3% a year (1926–2006); the −1.5% green-minus-brown wedge of Pástor, Stambaugh and Taylor (2022); Bolton and Kacperczyk's carbon results, which need industry controls; the confound that low-carbon firms in 2009–2020 were tech winners; and green bond spreads (Baker et al., Zerbib, Larcker and Watts), where finite duration eases the omitted-factor worry. On portfolios: Hartzmark and Sussman's Morningstar natural experiment, impact VC underperforming (Barber, Morse and Yasuda), and the activist exception (Dimson, Karakaş and Li) explained by buying brown, reforming and selling green.

Tests 3 to 5, who holds and who decides. Endowments and pensions screen, hedge funds hold brown. Surveys and experiments: the 4.57% willingness to pay found by Heeb et al., and what framing does to such estimates. Managerial motives: Cheng, Hong and Shue's 2003 dividend tax cut design against the contrary evidence of Ferrell, Liang and Renneboog. This is agency theory applied to CSR.

Tests 6 and 7, votes and indices. Explain the regression discontinuity at the 50% vote threshold, why Flammer (2015) and Cheng, Hong and Shue disagree, and the authors' new ISS sample (13,343 proposals, 2006–2021) with local means, then Lee–Lemieux polynomials, Carhart abnormal returns and firm-clustered errors. Result: just passing an SRI proposal adds no value, and just passing a governance proposal lowers the net ESG score by up to 1.5 points (0.77 standard deviations). Index inclusion shows no effect because a cash-flow effect (the numerator) and a discount-rate effect (the denominator) cancel.

Greeniums and real investment. Explain how equity and bond premia become a WACC wedge (debt to equity of 1, a 35% tax rate, a −6 basis point median bond premium), giving a mean of −1.41% with a standard deviation of 0.86%. Then invert the wedge: with observed q, back out m, multiply it by the share of assets held under green mandates, and compare the result with BloombergNEF decarbonisation spending. Predicted investment is too large, and the article gives five reasons: weak identification of willingness to pay, gaps in the spending data, greenwashing, idiosyncratic risk (Broccardo, Hart and Zingales) and warm glow.

For the green-finance listener. State what follows: green tilts most likely cost returns and are justified only by impact. Real effects need plant-level evidence, such as emissions cut through abatement capital spending versus through lower output (Akey and Appel), and rating disagreement across vendors weakens every test. Note the article's limits: US data, MSCI-KLD scores, governance mixed into the ESG measure, and a disclosed advisory role at an asset manager. Close by saying what the article does not answer (non-US markets, carbon pricing, transition risk hedging) and hand over to episode three, the quant showdown.
