Explain and discuss at graduate depth (1/3) Topic 3 of Corporate Finance, asymmetric information — concepts: what goes wrong in financial markets when one side knows more than the other. This is the first of three episodes: the second derives the results, the third works through the exercises and misconceptions. Sources: `Aula_Topic3 - Asymmetric information.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole, Ch. 6) and `Livro_de Matos, João Amaro - Theoretical Foundations of Corporate Finance (2002, Princeton University Press) [10.1515_9780691188478] - libgen.li.pdf` (hereafter Amaro de Matos, Ch. 3 and 6). No arithmetic on air. State each idea, its assumptions and where it breaks. Listeners know NPV and the Modigliani–Miller benchmark from Topics 2A and 2B.

Hidden information: used cars, insurance, workers, borrowers, IPO investors. Name the four consequences on the slides — adverse selection, cross-subsidisation, overinvestment by bad types, and underinvestment, up to market breakdown — and say which are about efficiency and which only about who pays.

The lemons model of credit: a penniless borrower, a project that pays or fails, risk-neutral competitive lenders, two privately known types with different success probabilities. Under symmetric information each type borrows at its own fair rate and keeps its NPV, and only positive-NPV projects run. Explain why this cannot survive asymmetric information (the bad type claims to be good), why one pooling rate emerges, and the three outcomes: lending with cross-subsidy and efficiency, lending with overinvestment by the bad type, or breakdown when good types are too rare. Stress who loses: the good type, through the presence of bad types and the suspicion they create.

More information: a credit bureau that records past defaults. Explain how a public signal splits the market into two pools, why it reduces cross-subsidy, and the key subtlety from the slides: it raises total value only if it stops lending to negative-NPV types; otherwise it only redistributes.

The pecking order: internal funds first, then debt, then equity, because debt is the least information-sensitive claim. Use Holmström's pawnshop: with enough collateral, private information about its value stops mattering.

Stiglitz and Weiss: raising the rate drives safe borrowers out first, so the lender's expected return can fall as the rate rises; supply bends backward and credit is rationed in equilibrium even though lenders could charge more.

Signalling: a good type can separate only with an action that costs the bad type more. Costly collateral is the slide example; name the others (payout, short maturity, retaining a large stake). Evidence: Kawai et al. (2022) on an online lending market, where adverse selection destroys a large share of surplus and signalling recovers most of it; Michaely et al. (2021), where payout signals cash-flow volatility, not its level.

Self-financing as a signal (Leland and Pyle, 1977; Amaro de Matos §3.2.2): a risk-averse owner would like to sell the whole firm, but keeping a stake is credible because it is cheaper for a good owner.

IPO underpricing (Rock; Amaro de Matos §6.2.2): informed investors buy only the good issues, so uninformed investors are rationed on good issues and get all of the bad ones — the winner's curse — and must be compensated by an offer price below expected value.

Close by stating what episode two derives: the breakdown threshold, the good type's discount, Bayes updating with a bureau, why debt minimises the lemons cost, the Stiglitz–Weiss cutoffs, the separating collateral contract, Leland–Pyle and the IPO offer price.
