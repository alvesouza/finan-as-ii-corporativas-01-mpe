Explain and discuss at graduate depth (1/3) Topic 4B of Corporate Finance, asset substitution, debt overhang and debt renegotiation — concepts: what goes wrong once risky debt is in place, and what renegotiation can and cannot repair. This is the first of three episodes: the second derives the results, the third works through the exercises and misconceptions. Sources: `Aula_Topic4B - Asset substitution, debt overhang and renegotiation.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole, §3.3) and `Livro_de Matos, João Amaro - Theoretical Foundations of Corporate Finance (2002, Princeton University Press) [10.1515_9780691188478] - libgen.li.pdf` (hereafter Amaro de Matos, §3.1.3). No arithmetic on air. State each idea, its assumptions and where it breaks. Listeners know the hidden-action model of Topic 4A and that equity is a call on the firm's assets (Topic 2A).

Asset substitution. Equity is a call on the assets with strike equal to the face value of debt, so shareholders keep the upside and walk away from the downside; debt is a safe bond minus a put, so creditors care only about the first dollars. Shareholders control the project choice, so with high leverage they tilt towards risk even when it lowers firm value. Tell the slide story of a firm that holds cash and owes a fixed amount, choosing among a value-maximising project, a risky value-destroying one that shareholders prefer, and a safe one that creditors prefer. Then the financing twist: new lenders anticipate the choice and refuse to lend, or lend only at a punishing rate; outside equity removes the conflict because inside and outside shareholders hold the same claim.

Implications and remedies: firms whose managers have wide discretion over risk should borrow less; distressed firms gamble for resurrection and delay bankruptcy. Remedies: reputation for future borrowing (the Jensen–Meckling quote), short-term debt, covenants, convertibles. Evidence: Gilje (2016) finds oil and gas firms cut risk as they approach distress. The underlying friction is commitment: no contract can pin down future project choice.

Debt overhang (Myers, 1977; Tirole §3.3). Debt in place acts as a tax on new investment: shareholders pay the full cost of a good project but part of the gain goes to creditors, whose claim becomes safer. The tax is heavier the closer the firm is to default. Household evidence: homeowners with negative equity spend less on maintenance (Melzer, 2017); debt relief changes labour supply (Dobbie and Song, 2020; Di Maggio et al., 2020).

Renegotiation as a fix: cutting the face value in exchange for the investment can leave both sides better off. Remedies before and after the fact: new senior claims and debtor-in-possession financing after the fact; bank debt and fewer creditors, which are easier to renegotiate, before it.

Benefits of renegotiation: ex post efficiency, insurance against bad luck for households, preserved access to credit (Bos et al., 2018), avoided foreclosure externalities (Campbell et al., 2011); most restructuring happens out of court (Dawsey et al., 2013), and Brazilian central-bank data show millions of over-indebted borrowers. Policy cases: the CARES Act, HAMP, student-debt cancellation.

Costs: the prospect of renegotiation makes debt soft, weakens incentives at the moral-hazard stage and invites strategic default (Mayer et al., 2014), like the too-big-to-fail problem. Ex post efficient, ex ante inefficient.

Default as insurance (Indarte, 2023): more generous debt relief insures the risk-averse borrower but raises default rates and so interest rates. Finally, Karlan and Zinman (2009): a randomised field experiment that separates adverse selection from moral hazard in consumer credit.

Close by stating what episode two derives: the example's payoffs and prices, the overhang wedge and the renegotiated face value, the default threshold and its comparative statics, and the Karlan–Zinman identification.
