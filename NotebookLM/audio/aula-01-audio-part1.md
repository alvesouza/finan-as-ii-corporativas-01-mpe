Explain and describe in detail (1/2) Class 1 of Corporate Finance: what the field studies, corporate governance, and the stylized facts of corporate financing. Using the uploaded sources `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole; all pages are printed pages) and `Programa_Finanças II.pdf` (the syllabus), this episode builds the conceptual half of the class: who the insiders and outsiders are, what can go wrong between them, what instruments contain it, and which facts about financing any theory must match. Graduate level: name every author, table and number, and argue, do not summarize. Part 2 will prove Modigliani–Miller and solve the credit-rationing model, so state the facts here as puzzles and leave their resolution for Part 2.

The two sides of the firm (Tirole Introduction, pp. 1–2): insiders run the firm, outsiders fund it. Explain why the Arrow–Debreu model, for all its power in pricing claims, "said little about the firms' financial choices and about their governance": when every action is contractible there is nothing to govern. Frame the field as a principal–agent problem and separate the three asymmetries by timing: adverse selection before the contract, hidden knowledge of realized income after the outcome, moral hazard in between. Give one startup example of each and flag moral hazard as the one Part 2 formalizes.

Separation of ownership and control (Tirole §1.1, pp. 15–16): Berle and Means (1932). Explain why monitoring is a public good among dispersed shareholders, with a numerical free-rider argument. Discuss the Shleifer–Vishny definition Tirole adopts, governance as the ways suppliers of finance "assure themselves of getting a return on their investment", and why it is written from the outsiders' side. Contrast the two shapes of the agency problem: dispersed owners against a manager, versus a controlling shareholder against minority shareholders, and ask who monitors the controller.

Moral hazard comes in many guises (§1.1.1, pp. 16–17): insufficient effort, extravagant investments (Jensen on oil exploration), entrenchment, self-dealing (RJR Nabisco's fleet, Tyco). Identify the common thread: a non-contractible action that shrinks what is left for outsiders. Then the evidence of dysfunctional governance (pp. 17–20): CEO pay growth, pay-for-luck (Bertrand–Mullainathan), WorldCom. Explain Tirole's iceberg: observed scandals are the selected tip, and few scandals can mean either good governance or little outside finance. Treat this as a selection-bias warning about reading governance evidence.

Instruments (§1.2, pp. 20–28): compensation, implicit incentives, monitoring, product-market competition. Walk through Jensen–Murphy: 3.25 dollars per 1,000 dollars of shareholder value, the grocer "eating the apples", and the Hall–Liebman revision. Compare a share paying P with an option paying max(0, P − strike): more incentive per unit of cost near the top, but under-water options invite gambling for resurrection, and repricing undoes the promise. Discuss why too large a stake hurts a risk-averse manager. Explain Gilson's evidence that distress triggers management turnover, so debt disciplines. Define active monitoring (forward-looking, control rights) versus speculative monitoring (backward-looking, prices and exit), and why monitors need incentives too.

Stylized facts (Tirole §2.5, pp. 95–102): read Table 2.2 (Mayer): retentions dominate in every country, loans are the bulk of external finance, new shares are small. Then net equity issuance negative in the US mid-1980s (p. 97), and Tirole's caution that retentions are also shareholders' money, so the real puzzle is why leaving profits in is easier than buying new shares. Table 2.4: typical debt–equity near 2, banks near 11, and who borrows (steady cash flow, resaleable assets). Table 2.5: bank-based versus market-based systems. Table 2.6: announcement returns of −3.1% for common stock, −2.0% for convertibles, insignificant for straight bonds, positive for bank loans. The cyclical facts on p. 101 and equity market timing.

Close on §2.6 (p. 102): information and incentives, capital, liquidity, collateral, monitoring. Map each fact to the syllabus topic in `Programa_Finanças II.pdf` that will explain it, and end with the open question for Part 2: if financing were irrelevant, why would any of these regularities exist?

Address misconceptions explicitly: governance is not "how the board is organised"; internal finance is not free money; a stock option is not simply a cheaper share; few scandals are not evidence of good governance.
