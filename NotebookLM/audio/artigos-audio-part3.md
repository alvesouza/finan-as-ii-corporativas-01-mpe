No arithmetic on air, and no code read aloud: describe each test's logic in words.

The static model. A firm with cash w and a concave project f(I) faces a convex cost of raising e, as in Froot, Scharfstein and Stein and in Kaplan and Zingales. The first order condition is f'(I) = 1 + C'(e), so the marginal value of cash, P_w = 1 + C'(e), is above one whenever the firm is constrained. The first best is independent of w, which is Modigliani–Miller. Explain why the sensitivity of investment to cash is nonlinear and nonmonotonic, which is why investment–cash flow regressions mislead.

The dynamic model. In Bolton, Chen and Wang (2011) q-theory with frictions: the HJB equation; a payout boundary pinned down by a super contact condition, where the marginal value of cash equals one; an equity issuance boundary with fixed and proportional costs; a credit line limit. Explain the striking result that in the credit region marginal q falls while investment rises, because investment follows marginal q divided by the marginal value of cash. Explain why hedging and asset sales are complements, and the corrected net tax benefit of debt, which reduces to Miller's formula only when the firm is unconstrained and can turn negative.

The asset-pricing core. Conditional beta equals (ρσ/σ_m) times p'(w)/p(w). Contrast it with the textbook unlevering formula that treats cash as negative debt: it underestimates beta by 24% starting from the payout boundary and overestimates it by 32% starting from zero cash, and beta at the boundary can fall to 81% of the unconstrained firm's. Explain how idiosyncratic volatility changes priced beta, with opposite signs near zero cash and far from it. Explain why endogenous cash can make high-cash firms earn more: Palazzo's 0.69% monthly equal-weighted spread between top and bottom deciles, the positive link between cash and credit spreads (Acharya, Davydenko and Strebulaev), cash-rich firms outperforming in late 2007 (Duchin, Ozbas and Sensoy) and early 2020 (Ramelli and Wagner), the financing-shock premium, and market timing (issuance and buybacks move together).

The backtest in words. Say how a quant would test these claims: sort on a cash-to-assets measure with reporting lags; separate equal and value weights; control for size, value and profitability; treat crises as conditional payoffs. List the traps: small-cap concentration, endogeneity of cash, look-ahead bias, state dependence. Discuss whether a high-cash long leg behaves like insurance (paying off when financing conditions break), and label that reading as the hosts' inference from the crisis evidence, not a result the article proves.

The challenger. Benmelech: in the DealScan medians by year (his Figure 2), raw secured spreads exceed unsecured ones by 150–200 basis points because of selection (Berger and Udell; Holmström's pawnshop from Topic 3). Identification comes from the intensive margin (airline redeployability: 58–64 basis points from the 25th to the 75th percentile), Sweden's 2004 floating-lien reform, and firm-by-time fixed effects. The secured premium is about 2 basis points for investment grade, 55 below it, and about 129 in secondary trading, rising by 92, 21 and 131 basis points from BBB to BB, BB to B and B to CCC. Security is used contingently, and the costs are lender hold-up and lost flexibility. Treat "a new secured issue signals distress" as the listener's hypothesis, not a tested result.
