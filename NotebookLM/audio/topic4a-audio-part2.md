Explain and discuss at graduate depth (2/3) Topic 4A of Corporate Finance, moral hazard, credit rationing and debt versus equity — the derivations. Listeners heard episode one (credit rationing, the hidden-action model, pledgeable income, the commitment problem, investment–cash-flow sensitivity, debt versus equity); episode three works through the exercises. Sources: `Aula_Topic4A - Moral hazard and credit rationing.pdf` (the slides) and `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole, §3.2 for the fixed-investment model, §3.4 for variable investment, §3.5–3.6 for debt as the optimal outside claim). No arithmetic on air. Derive each result in words, state its assumptions, and say where it breaks.

The incentive constraint: the entrepreneur behaves if the gain in success probability times her stake in success is at least the private benefit, so her stake must be at least the private benefit divided by that gain. Subtract it from the success payoff to get the pledgeable income per success, and multiply by the high success probability to get expected pledgeable income.

The participation constraint: lenders break even if the expected repayment covers what they lend. Put the two together: financing happens if and only if expected pledgeable income covers the amount borrowed, which is the same as net worth above a threshold. Read the threshold as the agency rent minus the project's NPV, and explain why the model assumes it positive.

Equilibrium: competition drives lenders to zero profit, so the repayment equals the loan divided by the high success probability, the rate is the same for all funded borrowers, and the entrepreneur captures the whole NPV. Then the rationing argument: take a borrower below the threshold who offers the going rate; her remaining stake falls short of the minimum, she misbehaves, and the lender loses, so lenders refuse rather than raise the rate. Comparative statics of the threshold: rising in the private benefit, falling in the likelihood ratio. Show that pledging wealth as collateral, with no liquidation loss, gives the same threshold.

The first best: with an observable action the contract simply specifies it, no incentive constraint, no rationing.

Two positive outcomes. In the first best, MM holds: safe debt up to the failure payoff and risky debt above it, or a share of equity, or any mix, all raise the same funds and the entrepreneur gets the NPV. With moral hazard, the incentive constraint concerns the difference between her reward in success and in failure. Write expected pledgeable income as the total project payoff, minus the high probability times the minimum incentive spread, minus whatever she gets in failure. So the reward in failure should be zero, which requires debt with face value at least the failure payoff.

All-equity financing: with a retained equity share the entrepreneur also gets a slice of the failure payoff, so the retained share needed for incentives is larger, investors can hold less, and the wealth threshold for equity finance exceeds the one for debt. Every project financed with equity can be financed with debt, not the reverse. The solution is not unique: risky debt alone, or riskless debt at the failure payoff plus outside equity.

Limits: with a risk-averse entrepreneur this high-powered scheme imposes too much risk; leverage also raises expected distress costs.
