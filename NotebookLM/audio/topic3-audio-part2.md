Explain and discuss at graduate depth (2/3) Topic 3 of Corporate Finance, asymmetric information — the derivations. Listeners heard episode one (lemons in credit, credit bureaux, the pecking order, Stiglitz–Weiss, signalling, Leland–Pyle, IPO underpricing); episode three works through the exercises. Sources: `Aula_Topic3 - Asymmetric information.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole, §6.2–6.3) and `Livro_de Matos, João Amaro - Theoretical Foundations of Corporate Finance (2002, Princeton University Press) [10.1515_9780691188478] - libgen.li.pdf` (hereafter Amaro de Matos, §3.2 and §6.2). No arithmetic on air. Derive each result in words, state its assumptions, and say where it breaks.

Pooling. Lenders price with the average success probability, a weighted mean of the two types. Derive the breakdown condition: lending stops when the average probability times the success payoff falls short of the investment, which defines a threshold share of good types. Show why the pooled repayment lies between the two fair repayments, and decompose the good type's payoff into her symmetric-information NPV minus a discount that grows with the share of bad types and the gap between the types. Show that the slide's form, with chi equal to the share of bad types times the gap in probabilities over the good type's probability, and the discount chi over one minus chi times the investment, is the same as the investment times the good type's probability over the average minus one.

The credit bureau. Walk through Bayes' rule: a clean record raises the posterior that the borrower is good, a bad record lowers it. Each pool gets its own break-even rate. Explain why ex ante value is unchanged when both types were already funded, and rises when the bad-record pool is no longer funded and its money goes to the safe asset.

The pecking order. With a payoff in both states, write the good type's utility as NPV plus a term proportional to the gap between the market's and her own success probability, times the spread between what investors get in success and in failure. That spread is the information-sensitive part; it is smallest when investors take the whole failure payoff, i.e. riskless debt up to the failure payoff and the rest as risky debt. Bad types pool, so both issue debt.

Stiglitz–Weiss. With two types of equal mean and different risk, derive the two cutoff rates: above the first the safe type drops out, above the second nobody borrows. The lender's expected return jumps down at the first cutoff, so it is not monotonic in the rate.

Costly collateral. Collateral worth less to the lender than to the borrower is pure waste under symmetric information. Under asymmetric information set up the good type's programme: maximise her payoff subject to the bad type not mimicking and the lender breaking even. Argue that both constraints bind, that the bad type posts nothing, and that signalling beats pooling when adverse selection is severe.

Leland–Pyle (Amaro de Matos §3.2.2). CARA utility and normal returns give a certainty-equivalent cost of retained risk. Show why the bad type sells everything, derive the retained share that makes her indifferent, why a continuum of separating equilibria exists, and why the least-cost one is Pareto-dominant; the good type's informational cost is the risk premium on the retained stake.

Rock. Uninformed investors get only a fraction of good issues and all of bad ones. Their break-even condition gives an offer price below the uninformed expected value. Derive the comparative statics: no informed investors, no underpricing; more informed capital or a wider value gap, more underpricing.
