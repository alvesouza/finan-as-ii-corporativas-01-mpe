The termination contract has three terms: the first-period repayment, the probability of continuing if it is not paid, and the probability of continuing if it is. Two assumptions: liquidation is inefficient (resale value below expected second-period income) and the resale value alone cannot repay the loan. The successful borrower repays if the gain in continuation probability times the value of continuing at least equals the repayment.

The programme: maximise the borrower's expected payoff subject to the lender breaking even, the truth-telling constraint and limited liability. Walk through the three claims as the slides prove them, by perturbation. First, the lender's constraint binds; with it slack, a small cut in the repayment helps the borrower. Second, continuation after repayment is certain: raising it and the repayment together, by the resale value per unit, keeps the lender whole and the borrower honest, and gains the difference between continuation value and resale value. Third, the truth-telling constraint binds: raising the continuation probability after default, and the repayment just enough to compensate the lender, again gains that difference.

Solve the two binding equations for the liquidation probability after default: the loan divided by a weighted average of the continuation value and the resale value, weighted by the probability of first-period success. Read the comparative statics off that expression and explain each through incentives or the lender's break-even.

Sovereign debt. State variables: the country's bond position, negative when it borrows, and current output. The value of having the option is the larger of defaulting and repaying. Repaying means consuming output plus maturing assets minus the cost of new bonds, plus the discounted option value next period; defaulting means consuming the reduced output now, with re-entry next period with some probability. The default set at a debt level is the set of outputs where default wins; next period's default probability is the probability of landing in it; the zero-profit bond price is one minus that probability, discounted at the world rate. A recursive equilibrium makes consumption feasible, policies optimal given prices, and prices consistent with default.

The properties, proved as on the slides. The repayment value rises with assets, because a richer country can copy any choice of a poorer one; so default sets shrink as assets rise and bond prices rise with assets. In the i.i.d. case with permanent exclusion and no output loss: if default is ever optimal at a debt level, no contract gives the country a net inflow, since otherwise it would borrow to repay and keep the option. Default incentives are stronger at low output, because repayment costs more utility when consumption is low, so the default set is all outputs below a threshold. The price of new debt then depends only on its size; the total the country can raise peaks at a finite debt level, an endogenous credit limit, and debt small enough never to be defaulted on is priced risk-free.
