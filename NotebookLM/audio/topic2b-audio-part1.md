Explain and discuss at graduate depth (1/3) Topic 2B of Corporate Finance, taxes, financial distress and the static trade-off — concepts: the first departures from Modigliani–Miller and the evidence on them. This is the first of three episodes: the second derives the valuation formulas and the trade-off condition, the third works through the exercises and misconceptions. Sources: `Aula_Topic2B - Taxes, distress and trade-off.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Berk DeMarzo Harford - Fundamentals of Corporate Finance 2e (2011).pdf` (hereafter Berk, Ch. 16–17), `Livro_Grinblatt Titman - Financial Markets and Corporate Strategy 2e (2002).pdf` (hereafter Grinblatt–Titman, Ch. 13–16) and `Livro_de Matos, João Amaro - Theoretical Foundations of Corporate Finance (2002, Princeton University Press) [10.1515_9780691188478] - libgen.li.pdf` (hereafter Amaro de Matos, Ch. 2). No arithmetic on air. State each idea, its assumptions and where it breaks. Listeners know MM I, MM II and unlevering from Topic 2A.

The tax friction: interest is deductible for the firm, while payments to shareholders are not. Argue why, with the same operating cash flow, the levered firm pays its investors more in total each period, why the whole gain goes to the shareholders who make the financing decision, and why homemade leverage cannot copy it: an investor who borrows personally gets no corporate deduction.

How risky is the tax shield? If the amount of debt is fixed, the shield is as safe as the debt; if the firm keeps a target debt-to-equity ratio, the debt moves with firm value and the shield becomes as risky as the assets. Explain why this choice decides the rate at which the shield is discounted, and so the formulas of episode two.

Personal taxes, extending the slides with Miller (1977): interest is taxed at the investor level, often more heavily than equity income, so the corporate advantage of debt can shrink and, in Miller's equilibrium, vanish, as firms issue debt until the marginal investor is indifferent. Add DeAngelo–Masulis: other deductions, such as depreciation, make the marginal value of interest deductions fall as debt rises, because the firm may not have enough taxable income to use them (Amaro de Matos §2.2, Grinblatt–Titman Ch. 14).

Financial distress. The transfer of control from shareholders to creditors in default is not by itself a violation of MM; deadweight costs are. Separate direct costs (lawyers, courts, administration) from indirect costs (lost customers, suppliers and employees, fire sales, management distraction). Judge the evidence: car prices and default risk (Hortaçsu et al., 2013); job applicants and talent (Brown and Matsa, 2016; Baghai et al., 2021); unemployment insurance as a subsidy to risky firms (Van Doornik et al., 2022). Then the agency costs of debt: risk-shifting, where shareholders gain from riskier projects, and debt overhang, where they turn down good ones; episode two derives both.

Bank capital: judge the three claims discussed by Admati et al.: that more equity lowers return on equity, that equity is expensive, and that it loses the tax shield. Say which two MM II answers and why, and separate the private cost in the third (the lost shield is a transfer from taxpayers) from the social benefit of fewer bank failures.

Payout policy: dividends and buybacks as two ways to distribute cash, why in a perfect market neither changes shareholder wealth, and the two forces that break this: taxes on dividends (Berk §17.3, Grinblatt–Titman Ch. 15) and signalling (Berk §17.5), which opens the asymmetric-information topic.

Close by stating what episode two will derive: the value of the tax shield, WACC versus APV, risk-shifting and debt overhang, and the trade-off first-order condition.
