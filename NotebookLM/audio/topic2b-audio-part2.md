No arithmetic on air. Derive each result in words, state its assumptions, and say where it breaks.

The baseline: with perpetual, safe debt, each year's shield is the tax rate times the interest; discounting at the debt rate gives a present value equal to the tax rate times the debt, so the levered firm is worth the unlevered firm plus that amount. Edge case from the slide appendix: with risky debt, the shield follows the promised coupon, which is paid only in solvency, not the expected return on debt.

The three closed forms with growth. Fixed debt: discount the shields at the debt rate. A debt-to-equity ratio kept constant continuously: the shields share the assets' risk, so discount them at the unlevered return. Annual rebalancing: the next shield is known one period ahead, so discount it one period at the debt rate and the rest at the unlevered return. Say what each implies for the levered cost of equity.

WACC versus APV. Retrace the appendix recursion: the firm's free cash flow plus the tax shield, discounted at the pre-tax WACC, equals free cash flow discounted at the after-tax WACC, and the two coincide when the debt-to-equity ratio is constant. Then say exactly when one WACC fails (a debt schedule that changes over time, such as a leveraged buyout paying down debt), and why APV handles any known debt schedule and can add the present value of distress costs. Link the slides' constant-debt WACC to the MM adjusted cost of capital, the unlevered return times one minus the tax rate times the debt-to-value ratio.

Miller's equilibrium, in words: write the gain from leverage with corporate and personal taxes, and show it is zero when one minus the personal tax on interest equals one minus the corporate rate times one minus the personal tax on equity income, so firms in aggregate are indifferent and only the economy's total debt is pinned down (Amaro de Matos §2.2).

Risk-shifting and debt overhang from Topic 2A's option view. Equity is a call on the assets, and a call's value rises with risk; so near default, shareholders gain from a riskier project even if it lowers firm value, at the creditors' expense (Grinblatt–Titman Ch. 16). Debt overhang: when new equity funds a project, the senior claim takes part of the value it creates, so shareholders reject positive-NPV projects; this is the bridge to Tirole Ch. 3.

The trade-off first-order condition. Raise the face value of debt by one unit: the marginal benefit is the tax rate times the probability of being solvent, when the shield is used; the marginal cost is the bankruptcy cost times the probability density at the default threshold. Sign the effects of a higher tax rate and a higher bankruptcy cost on optimal debt. Contrast with the two-state version. Explain why the frictionless-tax prediction of 100% debt fails, and what Jagannathan et al. (2017) and Graham et al. (2015) show about actual leverage.

Dividend irrelevance from sources equal uses: given investment and borrowing, a higher dividend must be financed by issuing shares at a fair price, so old shareholders' wealth is unchanged; then show which step fails with dividend taxes and with signalling.
