The asset-substitution example. Everyone is risk neutral, so every claim is valued at its expected payoff discounted at the risk-free rate. Split each project's payoff state by state into the creditors' part, the smaller of the payoff and the face value, and the shareholders' part, the excess. Show why the risky project with the fattest boom state maximises equity value although it has negative NPV, why creditors favour the safe one, and why the market-value balance sheet records the loss the moment the choice is anticipated. Then the new-financing case: if the shareholders' preferred project destroys value, no face value lets lenders recover their money; if it creates some value, lenders raise the face value until their claim, given the risky choice, is worth what they lend. With outside equity the insiders keep a share of firm value, so they maximise firm value.

The friction: the slides relate it to Holmström–Tirole; in both, shareholders cannot commit and pay for it through denied credit or a higher rate.

Debt overhang. Value assets in place, debt and equity state by state when the face value exceeds the slump value. A project that adds the same amount in both states has positive NPV, but in the slump the whole gain goes to creditors, so equity rises by less than the cost. State the general wedge: the share of the gain lost to creditors equals the probability-weighted part paid in default states, which is why overhang worsens near default.

Renegotiation. Cut the face value conditional on the investment: equity's gain must cover the cost and creditors must end up above their status-quo value, which brackets the feasible new face values; bargaining power picks one. Explain why, in the slide example, an unconditional cut works too: shareholders are indifferent between investing and not after the cut. Explain why junior new debt cannot cure overhang while senior new money (debtor-in-possession) can.

Default as insurance (Indarte, 2023). The borrower repays if consumption after repayment beats the protected consumption in default minus its non-pecuniary cost. With income in a range, a threshold income exists below which she defaults, partly strategically. Differentiate: the default probability rises with the protected amount, because the threshold moves up, weighted by the ratio of marginal utilities.

Karlan and Zinman (2009). Three stages: take-up at the offer rate, a surprise lower contract rate and a random promise of cheaper future loans, then repayment. Derive from the effort first-order condition that effort falls with the contract rate and rises with the cost of default; from take-up, that a higher offer rate attracts riskier types. The regression: the offer-rate coefficient identifies selection, the contract-rate and future-rate coefficients identify hidden action. State what it cannot separate: selection on risk from selection on anticipated effort.
