Explain and discuss at graduate depth (1/3) Topic 5 of Corporate Finance, nonverifiable income, the threat of termination and sovereign debt — concepts: how lending survives when the lender cannot see, or cannot seize, what the borrower earns. This is the first of three episodes: the second derives the results, the third works through the exercises and misconceptions. Sources: `Aula_Topic5 - Nonverifiable income and sovereign debt.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole, §3.8 on nonverifiable income) and `Livro_de Matos, João Amaro - Theoretical Foundations of Corporate Finance (2002, Princeton University Press) [10.1515_9780691188478] - libgen.li.pdf` (hereafter Amaro de Matos, §5.2.1–5.2.2). No arithmetic on air. State each idea, its assumptions and where it breaks. Listeners know the moral-hazard model of Topic 4A and the renegotiation trade-off of Topic 4B.

Nonverifiable income: the borrower can always claim the project failed. Give the slide readings: small firms whose sales leave no invoice, spending that is hard to tell from perks, and sovereign borrowers whose revenue and assets no court can seize. Distinguish a liquidity default, where the cash is not there, from a strategic default, where it is there but is not paid. Two ways to deter strategic default: direct costs such as seized collateral or penalties, and, in a repeated relationship, the threat to stop financing.

The threat of termination. In one period a lender who cannot verify income is never repaid, so the project is never funded, however profitable. Add a second period of income that exists only if the project continues: the lender cannot extract anything in the last period, but can make continuation depend on repayment in the first. Liquidation gives the lender a resale value but destroys more value than it recovers, so it is used as a punishment, randomly and only as often as needed to make repayment worthwhile. Explain the economics of the results: never liquidate after repayment, liquidate with some probability after default, and the comparative statics — the threat needs to be used less when the future is more valuable, the liquidation value higher, early success more likely and the borrower richer. Two readings: liquidation as costly collateral; and its fragility, since once termination is announced the borrower can bribe the lender with slightly more than the resale value, which undermines the threat ex ante.

Sovereign debt (Eaton and Gersovitz, 1981; Arellano, 2008). A small open economy with random output wants to smooth consumption by borrowing abroad in one-period bonds that cannot be enforced. Default is chosen whenever it pays; its costs are temporary exclusion from credit markets and lost output while excluded. Competitive, risk-neutral investors price bonds at the probability of repayment, so interest rates embed default risk and depend on how much the country borrows and on its output.

What the model delivers, in words: default is more tempting when debt is high, when output is low (repaying hurts more in a recession because utility is concave), and when the country cannot roll its debt over; bond prices fall as debt rises, which creates an endogenous borrowing limit, so in a bad year the country would like to borrow more but no lender offers the contract; and low debt is risk-free.

Close by stating what episode two derives: the optimal termination contract and its three claims, the probability of liquidation, the sovereign's value functions, the equilibrium price schedule, and why default happens in bad times.
