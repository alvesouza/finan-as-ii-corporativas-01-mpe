Explain and discuss at graduate depth (1/3) Topic 4A of Corporate Finance, moral hazard, credit rationing and the choice between debt and equity — concepts. This is the first of three episodes: the second derives the results, the third works through the exercises and misconceptions. Sources: `Aula_Topic4A - Moral hazard and credit rationing.pdf` (the slides) and `Livro_Jean Tirole - The Theory of Corporate Finance-Princeton University Press (2005).pdf` (hereafter Tirole, Ch. 3, §3.2 and §3.4–3.6). No arithmetic on air. State each idea, its assumptions and where it breaks. Listeners know the Modigliani–Miller benchmark and the adverse-selection model of Topic 3.

Why capital structure now matters: in the hidden-action model of Holmström and Tirole (1997), financing changes the borrower's behaviour, and so the asset side of the firm. Contrast this with MM, where financing only repackages given cash flows.

Credit rationing. In most markets price clears supply and demand; credit markets also clear through quantities. Use the definitions on the slides (Bester and Hellwig; Baltensperger): a borrower is rationed if refused a loan she is willing to pay for, price and non-price terms included; being refused for lack of collateral or cash flow is not rationing. Bring in De Mel et al. (2008): microenterprises in Sri Lanka earn monthly returns on capital far above market rates, which says money is not reaching high-return uses.

The model in words: an entrepreneur with some wealth but less than the investment cost, competitive risk-neutral lenders, a project that succeeds or fails, and an unobservable action: behave, which raises the success probability, or misbehave, which yields a private benefit. Give both readings of the private benefit, effort saved and a pet project (the Jensen–Meckling quote on perks). Limited liability means investors get nothing on failure, so a contract is just the split of the success payoff.

The conflict: lenders always want the high action; the entrepreneur weighs her share of the payoff against the private benefit. Assumptions make behaving efficient and misbehaving value-destroying, so any viable contract must make behaving worthwhile.

Skin in the game: the entrepreneur must keep a minimum stake in success; what is left for investors is the pledgeable income, and it does not depend on how much she borrows. Hence the central result: lenders fund only borrowers with enough net worth. Explain why poor borrowers are rationed even if they offer a higher rate (a higher repayment lowers her stake and invites misbehaviour), and why this is a commitment problem: she would like to promise to behave but cannot. Which parameters raise the wealth threshold, and why the informativeness of the outcome about the action matters. Wealth can equally be read as collateral.

Investment and cash flow: Fazzari and Petersen (1988) find investment responds to internal cash, more so for constrained firms. In the model only firms near the threshold respond, and a marginal unit of cash can be worth more than one.

Debt versus equity. Recall seniority and the residual claim. With only two outcomes, one of them zero, debt and equity coincide. With two positive outcomes they differ: equity rewards the entrepreneur even in failure, which weakens incentives; debt leaves her nothing in failure, which maximises the power of the incentive scheme and the pledgeable income. Debt is not a panacea: the result leans on risk neutrality and ignores distress costs, debt overhang and asset substitution, the subject of Topic 4B.

Close by stating what episode two derives: the incentive and participation constraints, the wealth threshold, the equilibrium rate, the rationing argument, and why debt dominates equity.
