Book exercises. Lista_R7 exercise 13 (Amaro de Matos 5.2.5 #1): in the Hart–Moore and Bolton–Scharfstein debt contract, the probability of continuing after default falls as the probability of success rises; connect it to the slide formula for the liquidation probability and explain why the two say the same thing. Exercise 15 (Amaro de Matos 5.2.5 #2, Bolton–Scharfstein 1996): two creditors are paid more than one after a strategic default but less after a liquidity default; explain why a harder-to-renegotiate debt structure deters strategic default at the cost of worse outcomes when the cash is really not there, and connect it to Topic 4B's advice to borrow from few creditors to ease renegotiation. Exercise 18 (Tirole 4.9): continuation decided on verifiable short-term profit, implemented by short-term debt; a court that gives the borrower half the liquidation proceeds ends up hurting her, the ex ante cost of ex post leniency.

No course book has an exercise on the sovereign model, so have the hosts pose and answer four slide-based questions in words. (1) Why does a one-period project with nonverifiable income never get funded, however profitable? (2) In the termination contract, why is it never optimal to liquidate a borrower who has just repaid? (3) Why, in the i.i.d. sovereign model, can a country that might default never be a net borrower in that period, and what does this say about rolling over debt? (4) Why do defaults cluster in recessions, and why does the country face a borrowing limit exactly when it most wants to borrow?

Name and correct four misconceptions, each with its mechanism: a borrower defaults only when she cannot pay (with nonverifiable income strategic default is the central problem); liquidation after default is pure waste and should be dropped (it is the cost of making repayment credible; without it there is no lending); renegotiation after a termination decision is harmless (a side payment just above the resale value saves the project ex post but empties the threat ex ante); sovereign defaults happen because countries borrow in good times and then cannot pay (in the model default is a choice, made when output is low and debt high, and the price schedule already charges for it).

Close by tying the module together: Topic 3 hid the borrower's type, Topic 4 her action, Topic 5 her income; in each case the contract trades efficiency for credibility, and debt — repayment or punishment — is the instrument that does it.
