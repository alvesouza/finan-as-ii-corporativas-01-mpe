## bs

Q: A firm with safe debt announces a positive-NPV project that will be paid for out of cash it generates later; no cash has been spent and no securities issued yet, and investors believe the announcement. What happens on the announcement day?
- Book equity rises by the NPV, since the project is a new asset the firm controls, while both the book and the market debt-equity ratios stay where they were.
- Debt and equity both rise in market value by a share of the NPV in proportion to their weights, since both are claims on the same pool of the firm's assets.
- Market equity rises by the NPV while book equity is unchanged, so market-to-book rises and the market debt-equity ratio falls with no change in the book one.
- Nothing changes in market or book values until the project's cash flows appear, since market values only reflect the cash already generated by the assets.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Market values are forward-looking: the moment investors expect the project, the value of the assets rises by its NPV. Safe debt has a fixed payoff, so the residual claim, equity, captures the whole gain (slide 29: a positive-NPV project enriches the current claimants). Book values are backward-looking and record nothing until a cost is incurred, and then only at cost. So market-to-book rises and market $D/E$ falls while book $D/E$ stays put — one reason the market ratio is the more informative leverage measure.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 8, 13, 22–23, 27–29; Topic 2A slides 28–31; Higgins 13e Ch. 1 §1.7.1 (p. 22)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 4 (Berk 2.8); Lista R1, Ex. 6 (Berk 2.21)

Q: In a frictionless market, a firm issues new debt and keeps all the proceeds as cash, with no change to its operations. What happens to its enterprise value (market equity plus debt minus cash), its net debt and its gross market debt-equity ratio?
- Enterprise value and net debt are unchanged, since the new debt is offset by the new cash, while the gross debt-equity ratio rises and overstates the true leverage.
- Enterprise value rises by the amount borrowed, since debt enters the formula with a plus sign, while net debt and the gross debt-equity ratio both rise by that amount.
- Enterprise value falls because equity becomes riskier and is discounted at a higher rate, while net debt is unchanged and the gross debt-equity ratio rises.
- Enterprise value is unchanged but net debt rises, since cash is an asset of the firm rather than a claim on it, so only the gross debt-equity ratio is left unaffected.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Enterprise value is the value of the operating assets: what a buyer must pay for all the equity and the debt, net of the cash he finds inside. Borrowing $\Delta$ and holding it raises debt and cash by $\Delta$ each, and in the frictionless benchmark the fair loan leaves market equity unchanged, so EV and net debt do not move. The gross ratio $D/E$ rises although the firm's net position is the same — the reason the slides treat cash as negative debt for cash-rich firms.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 29–30; Topic 2A slides 82, 101–102; Higgins 13e Ch. 9 §9.1.1 (p. 338)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 4(c) (Berk 2.8); Lista R1, Ex. 6 (Berk 2.21)

Q: A bank funds itself with short-term deposits and holds long-term fixed-rate bonds classified as held-to-maturity, carried at historical cost. Market interest rates then rise sharply; the bonds carry no default risk. What is correct?
- There is no economic loss, since the bonds will be held to maturity and repaid in full, so historical cost is the right measure and book equity tracks market equity.
- The rise in rates lowers the value of the deposits by the same amount as the bonds, since both are fixed claims, so the bank's market equity is roughly unchanged.
- Book equity falls at once by the decline in the bonds' market value, since accounting rules require every security to be marked to market whenever rates move.
- The bonds' market value falls while their book value does not, so book equity overstates market equity and book leverage understates the bank's true leverage.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> A fixed stream of payments is worth less when discount rates rise; holding to maturity does not undo the loss, it only spreads it as years of below-market yield. Short-term deposits reprice quickly, so their value barely moves and no offset arises. Held-to-maturity securities stay at historical cost, so book equity hides the loss — the Silicon Valley Bank case on slide 10, and why market leverage is what matters.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 10, 22–23, 28; Topic 2A slides 4–5; Higgins 13e Ch. 1 §1.7.1–1.7.2 (pp. 22–26)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 5(g) (Higgins 2.3); Lista R2b, Ex. 2 (Higgins 5.5)

## acc

Q: A fast-growing distributor reports rising net income every year, yet its cash flow from operating activities is negative year after year. Which explanation is consistent with how that line of the cash-flow statement is built?
- Its depreciation charges are large relative to earnings, and since depreciation is deducted from net income without a cash outlay it drags operating cash flow below income.
- Its receivables and inventories grow faster than its payables, so each year's sales and stock-building absorb cash that net income records as earned but not collected.
- Its capital expenditures exceed its depreciation, and since purchases of equipment are subtracted inside operating activities they push operating cash flow below zero.
- Its interest payments on growing debt are large, and since interest is paid in cash but left out of net income, operating cash flow falls short of reported earnings.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Operating cash flow starts from net income, adds back non-cash charges such as depreciation (which raises it above income, not below), deducts increases in receivables and inventory and adds increases in payables. A growing firm selling on credit and building stock invests in working capital every year, so profits accrue faster than cash arrives. Capex sits in investing activities, and interest is already inside net income.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 32–33, 52–55, 59; Higgins 13e Ch. 1 §1.1 (p. 3), §1.6 (p. 18); Ross *Fundamentos* Cap. 2 §2.4 (p. 37)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 10 (Higgins 1.11); Lista R1, Ex. 9 (Ross 2.14)

Q: A firm switches from straight-line to accelerated depreciation in both its tax return and its published statements, with no change in operations. In the first year, with tax rate $\tau$ and extra depreciation $\Delta$, what happens to net income and free cash flow?
- Net income falls by $(1-\tau)\Delta$ while free cash flow rises by $\tau\Delta$, because depreciation is not a cash cost but it does cut the taxes actually paid.
- Net income and free cash flow both fall by $(1-\tau)\Delta$, because the larger depreciation charge is an expense the firm deducts from its operating cash.
- Net income falls by $\Delta$ while free cash flow is unchanged, because depreciation is a non-cash entry that is added back in full when computing cash flow.
- Net income falls by $(1-\tau)\Delta$ and free cash flow falls by $\tau\Delta$, because the firm brings an expense forward and therefore pays its taxes earlier.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Net income falls by the after-tax charge, $(1-\tau)\Delta$. In $FCF = EBIT(1-\tau) + \text{Dep} - \text{Capex} - \Delta NWC$, EBIT falls by $\Delta$ and depreciation rises by $\Delta$, so $FCF$ changes by $-(1-\tau)\Delta + \Delta = \tau\Delta$: the depreciation tax shield. Faster depreciation brings tax savings forward, which raises value. Had the switch been made only in the published statements, cash flow would not move at all.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 16, 34–35, 54, 60; Topic 2A slides 24, 27; Higgins 13e Ch. 7 §7.3.1 (p. 260)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 3 (Berk 2.31); Lista R1, Ex. 13(a) (Berk 2.23)

## ratio

Q: Holding assets, sales and EBIT fixed, a firm replaces part of its equity with debt. How do the three DuPont factors and ROE move?
- Net margin is unchanged since EBIT is fixed, asset turnover is unchanged and the equity multiplier rises, so ROE rises with each extra unit of debt the firm issues.
- The fall in net margin from interest exactly offsets the rise in the equity multiplier, as MM implies, so ROE is unchanged whatever the interest rate on the new debt.
- Net margin falls, turnover is unchanged and the equity multiplier rises; ROE rises only if the return on invested capital exceeds the after-tax interest rate.
- Net margin falls and turnover rises because the asset base is now partly borrowed; ROE falls whenever the interest expense is larger than the firm's tax payment.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Net margin uses net income, which falls by the after-tax interest; turnover is sales over total assets, which did not change; assets/equity rises. Writing $ROIC = EBIT(1-\tau)/(D+E)$, one gets $ROE = ROIC + \frac{D}{E}\,[ROIC - r_D(1-\tau)]$, so leverage raises ROE only when ROIC beats the after-tax cost of debt — and even then the higher ROE comes with riskier equity, which is why ROE is a poor guide to value (Higgins's two-firm example: same ROIC, very different ROE).
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 46–50; Higgins 13e Ch. 2 §2.2.1 (p. 38), §2.3.2 (p. 54); Ross *Fundamentos* Cap. 3 (pp. 52–92, Du Pont identity)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 7 (Berk 2.27); Lista R1, Ex. 8 (Higgins 2.7)

Q: A firm's ROE has exceeded its cost of equity $r_E$ for years, and its stock trades well above book value. An investor concludes that buying its shares today will earn a return above $r_E$. Why is the conclusion wrong?
- Because ROE uses net income, which includes non-cash items, the cash return on the shares is always below ROE, so the buyer earns less than $r_E$ in expectation.
- Because a high ROE signals high leverage, and by MM II leverage lowers the expected return on equity, so the investor should expect a return below $r_E$.
- Because ROE is measured after taxes and $r_E$ before taxes, the two are not comparable, and adjusting for taxes would bring ROE back down to the cost of equity.
- Because ROE is earned on past book equity, while the price already capitalises those excess returns, so a buyer paying market value expects to earn only $r_E$.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> ROE above the hurdle rate says past investments, recorded at cost, earned more than their risk required — the economic-earnings idea of slide 42, and the reason market-to-book exceeds one. A buyer today pays the forward-looking market price, which already includes the value of those excess returns, so in a competitive market the expected return on that price is $r_E$. ROE is backward-looking and measured on book, not market, equity (slide 49). MM II says leverage raises $r_E$, not lowers it.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 22, 27, 42, 49; Topic 2A slides 4, 28–29; Higgins 13e Ch. 1 §1.7.3 (p. 27), Ch. 2 §2.3.3 (p. 56)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 5(f)–(g) (Higgins 2.3); Lista R1, Ex. 8(b) (Higgins 2.7)

Q: In a frictionless market with no taxes, an all-equity firm whose earnings yield (EPS over price) exceeds the interest rate on safe debt borrows and buys back shares at a fair price. What happens to EPS, the share price and the P/E ratio?
- EPS rises and, with the P/E ratio unchanged, the share price rises in proportion, which is why debt-financed buybacks create value for the remaining shareholders.
- EPS rises while the share price is unchanged, so the P/E ratio falls, reflecting the higher risk and higher required return on each remaining share of equity.
- EPS falls because the interest on the new debt is deducted from earnings, and the share price falls with it, so the P/E ratio stays where it was before the buyback.
- EPS and the share price both stay where they were, since MM says that capital structure is irrelevant for all accounting and market measures of the firm alike.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Retiring shares whose earnings yield exceeds the interest rate saves more earnings than the interest costs, so EPS rises. By MM I the firm's value is unchanged and, with a fair repurchase price, so is the share price. Price over a higher EPS gives a lower P/E: by MM II each share is now riskier and its required return is higher, which is the slide-51 point that P/E ratios are influenced by leverage. Holding P/E fixed is the same fallacy as holding $r_E$ fixed in the "borrow more" example.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 40, 51; Topic 2A slides 79–80, 97–99; Ross *Fundamentos* Cap. 16 §16.2 (p. 542)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 12(c) (Berk 2.19); Lista R1, Ex. 13 (Berk 2.23); Lista R3, Ex. 9 (G&T 13.15–13.16)

## fcf

Q: Next year a firm will borrow a large amount and pay all of it out as a special dividend; its operations are unaffected and interest is tax-deductible. How do next year's unlevered free cash flow (FCF) and free cash flow to equity (FCFE) respond, and what happens in later years?
- FCF is unchanged; FCFE rises next year by the net borrowing, and in later years FCFE is lower by the interest paid, net of the tax that the interest saves.
- FCF and FCFE both rise next year by the amount borrowed, since the cash raised is available for distribution, and both fall later as the debt is repaid with interest.
- FCF and FCFE are both unchanged, since financing never affects cash flows, and the only effect of the new debt shows up in the discount rate applied to the equity.
- FCF falls next year by the interest on the new debt, while FCFE rises by the amount borrowed, and in later years both are lowered by the full pre-tax interest bill.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> FCF is built from $EBIT(1-\tau)$ and ignores financing, so it does not move. FCFE is FCF minus after-tax interest plus net borrowing (slide 61): next year it jumps by the amount borrowed (the dividend), and afterwards it is lower by $(1-\tau)$ times the interest. That is why FCF values the enterprise and FCFE the equity, each at its own rate.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 60–62; Topic 2A slides 24–25; Higgins 13e Ch. 8 §8.6.1 (p. 307); Ross *Fundamentos* Cap. 2 §2.4 (p. 37)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 11(d) (Ross 2.21); Lista R1, Ex. 15 (Ross 2.25–2.26)

Q: In the FCF formula, the working-capital investment counts increases in current assets minus increases in non-interest-bearing current liabilities. Why are excess cash and short-term bank loans left out of that netting?
- Both are long-term items in economic substance, since banks routinely roll short-term loans over, so they belong with the capital expenditures and not with working capital.
- Excess cash earns interest that is taxed and bank loans charge interest that is deducted, so including them would make the tax on EBIT inconsistent with the taxes paid.
- Neither changes much from year to year in a well-run firm, so leaving them out only simplifies the computation and has no effect on the value obtained from FCF.
- Both are financing items: excess cash is negative debt, subtracted to reach net debt and EV, and the cost of bank loans enters through the discount rate, not the cash flow.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> FCF is the cash the operating assets generate for all investors. Payables and accruals are spontaneous, interest-free operating finance, so they reduce the working-capital investment. Bank loans are interest-bearing claims of investors: their cost belongs in the WACC, and putting them in the cash flow would count financing twice. Excess cash is a financial asset, treated as negative debt when moving from enterprise value to equity value.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 20, 29–30, 60; Topic 2A slides 24–25; Higgins 13e Ch. 7 §7.3.2 (p. 262), §7.3.7 (p. 267)
> Similar: Lista R1, Ex. 11(c) (Ross 2.21); Lista R2, Ex. 11 (Berk 9.25)
