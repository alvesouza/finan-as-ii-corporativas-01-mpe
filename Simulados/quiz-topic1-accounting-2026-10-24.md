## bs

Q: At end-2020 Starbucks reported book equity of about −7.9 bn USD while its market capitalisation was about 122.6 bn USD. Which reading of this gap is correct?
- Starbucks was insolvent in economic terms, and its market cap reflected a speculative premium that would vanish once creditors enforced their covenants.
- The negative book value came from goodwill write-downs, so restating goodwill at fair value would restore positive book equity and close most of the gap.
- Book equity records past costs and payouts, while market value prices future cash flows from assets the balance sheet omits, such as brand and loyal customers.
- Book equity was negative only because leases are booked as liabilities, so excluding operating leases would make book and market equity roughly equal.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Book values are backward-looking (historical cost, cumulative earnings less payouts); market values are forward-looking. Many valuable assets — reputation, customer loyalty, management quality — never appear on the balance sheet. Negative book equity says nothing about solvency when the market values the future cash flows highly.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 22–25; Higgins 13e Ch. 1 §1.7.1 (p. 22); Ross *Fundamentos* Cap. 2 §2.1 (p. 25)
> Similar: Higgins 13e Ch. 1 problems (p. 32); Topic 1 slide 28 (market debt-equity for negative-book firms)

Q: Why does the identity "assets = liabilities + equity" hold in market values, and not only as a bookkeeping rule?
- Every cash flow from the assets is promised either to creditors or to the owners as residual claimants, so the assets are worth the two claims together.
- Accountants mark every asset and liability to market at each reporting date, so the identity in market values is inherited directly from the book identity.
- Equity is defined as the plug that balances the sheet, so in market values it equals assets minus the face value of everything promised to creditors.
- Debt is issued at par in competitive markets, so the market value of liabilities always equals their book value and only equity ever needs to be revalued.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> The cash flows from assets are split between non-owners (senior promises) and owners (the residual): $FCF_A = FCF_L + FCF_E$, so their present values add up, $A = L + E$. Most items are not marked to market, and the market value of debt differs from its face value once rates or default risk change.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 8, 12–13; Tirole Ch. 2 §2.2 (p. 77)
> Similar: PS2 Ex. 1(e) (market values of $D$, $E$, $A$ for a given face value)

Q: Enterprise value is computed as market equity plus debt minus cash. Why is cash subtracted?
- Cash earns less than the cost of capital, so it destroys value over time and must be deducted to reflect the drag it imposes on the operating business.
- Cash is already counted twice in the market value of equity, once as an asset and once through retained earnings, so one of the two counts must be removed.
- Cash is pledged to creditors under most loan covenants, so a buyer of the equity cannot use it and must treat it as an additional liability of the firm.
- A buyer of all the equity who also repays the debt acquires the firm's cash as well, and can use it to pay down that debt, so cash acts like negative debt.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Enterprise value answers "how much does it cost to own the business assets outright?": buy all the equity, pay off the debt, and net out the cash you inherit. This is also why net debt (debt minus cash and marketable securities) is the better leverage measure for cash-rich firms.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 29–30; Higgins 13e Ch. 9 (pp. 335–384)
> Similar: Higgins 13e Ch. 9 problems (business valuation)

## is

Q: A firm books a large credit sale in December and the customer pays in February. How does the sale appear in the December statements?
- Revenue and an equal cash inflow are both recorded in December, because the matching principle requires cash and revenue to be recognised in the same period.
- Revenue is recorded in December and receivables rise; the cash-flow statement deducts that increase, so December operating cash flow excludes the sale.
- No revenue is recorded until February, because under the accrual principle revenue is recognised only once the customer's payment has actually cleared.
- Revenue is recorded in December, and the cash-flow statement adds the increase in receivables back, since receivables are a non-cash charge like depreciation.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Accrual accounting recognises revenue at the sale, not at payment. Net income therefore includes cash not yet received, and the operating section of the cash-flow statement corrects this by subtracting the rise in accounts receivable.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 32–33, 55; Higgins 13e Ch. 1 §1.4.1 (p. 12), §1.6 (p. 18)
> Similar: PS1 Ex. 1(a) (net income vs. free cash flow)

Q: The DuPont identity writes ROE as net margin × asset turnover × assets/equity. Should managers therefore push financial leverage as high as possible?
- No: more leverage raises ROE mechanically but also raises the risk borne by shareholders, and ROE ignores risk, so a higher ROE need not mean more value.
- Yes: ROE is the return on the shareholders' own money, so any rise in assets per unit of equity makes them richer as long as net income stays positive.
- No: leverage cannot raise ROE at all, since the interest expense lowers net income in exact proportion to the fall in equity, leaving the product unchanged.
- Yes, up to the covenant limit: lenders cap leverage exactly where the marginal gain in ROE from further borrowing falls to zero, which is the optimum.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> ROE is backward-looking, single-period, book-based, and blind to risk. Leverage scales ROE up, but the required return on equity rises too — the MM II logic of Topic 2A. A higher ROE from borrowing is a repackaging of risk, not value creation.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 47–49; Higgins 13e Ch. 2 §2.2.1 (p. 38), §2.3.2 (p. 54)
> Similar: Higgins 13e Ch. 2 problems (p. 71); PS2 Ex. 4(c) (EPS after a debt-financed repurchase)

Q: Two firms have identical operations and identical EBIT; one is financed partly with debt, the other only with equity. Which measure ranks them as equally good operators, and why?
- ROE, because it divides net income by equity, and equity is exactly what shareholders committed in both firms regardless of how much debt the first one carries.
- ROA, because it divides net income by total assets, and the two firms have identical assets, so any difference in net income is scaled by the same base.
- ROIC, because it divides after-tax EBIT by interest-bearing debt plus equity, removing interest from the numerator and putting all capital in the base.
- Net margin, because it divides net income by sales, and identical operations imply identical sales, so the financing mix cancels out of the ratio entirely.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> ROE and ROA both use net income, which is after interest, so they differ across the two firms even though operations are identical. ROIC $= EBIT(1-\tau)/(\text{debt} + \text{equity})$ strips out the financing choice and gives both firms the same return.
> Ref: Topic 1 slide 50; Higgins 13e Ch. 2 §2.3.2 (p. 54)
> Similar: Higgins 13e Ch. 2 problems (p. 71)

Q: Firm X trades at a P/E ratio well above that of its industry peers. Which inference is most defensible from this fact alone?
- X is more profitable than its peers, because the P/E rises one-for-one with net margin, so a higher multiple directly signals higher accounting earnings.
- X is less risky than its peers, because the P/E falls with growth prospects, so low risk is the only remaining reason for the higher multiple observed.
- X is overvalued, since firms of equal risk in one industry must trade at the same P/E, and arbitrageurs shorting the stock will soon close the gap.
- The market may expect faster growth or lower risk for X, or may be overpricing it; leverage also moves the P/E, so the ratio alone cannot tell which.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Growth prospects raise the P/E, risk lowers it, and capital structure shifts it. A high relative P/E is consistent with several stories, overvaluation among them; it does not identify any one of them.
> Ref: Topic 1 slide 51; Higgins 13e Ch. 2 §2.3.3 (p. 56)
> Similar: PS2 Ex. 4(d) (forward P/E after a change in leverage)

## cf

Q: Why does valuation discount free cash flows rather than a ready-made accounting figure such as net income?
- Net income is computed after taxes, while free cash flow is a pre-tax figure, and discount rates such as the WACC are defined only for pre-tax cash flows.
- Net income includes non-cash charges, omits capex and working-capital investment, and deducts interest, so it mixes operating results with financing.
- Net income is audited only once a year, while free cash flow is reported every quarter, which gives the analyst a more frequent series to discount.
- Net income includes depreciation, which double counts capex, while free cash flow leaves out both depreciation and capex so that neither enters value.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Investors care about the cash they can extract. Net income adds non-cash items (depreciation), ignores cash spent on capex and working capital, and is after interest — a financing flow that the discount rate handles. FCF is after tax and does subtract capex.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 52, 60; Topic 2A slide 24; Ross *Fundamentos* Cap. 2 §2.4 (p. 37)
> Similar: PS1 Ex. 1(a)

Q: An analyst forecasts both the unlevered free cash flow (FCF) and the free cash flow to equity (FCFE) of the same firm. Which pairing of cash flow and value is correct?
- FCF gives the value of equity, since it is the cash left after reinvestment, while FCFE gives enterprise value, since it adds back the debt the firm issues.
- Both give enterprise value, since FCFE differs from FCF only by after-tax interest, a financing flow that does not change the total value of the firm.
- FCF gives enterprise value, since it is available to creditors and owners alike; FCFE, net of after-tax interest and net borrowing, gives equity value.
- Both give the value of equity, since creditors receive a fixed amount, so whatever cash is left belongs to the owners whether or not interest is netted.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> FCF is the cash available to all capital providers after investment, so discounting it gives enterprise value. FCFE subtracts after-tax interest and adds net debt issuance, leaving what belongs to shareholders, so it gives equity value.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 60–62; Higgins 13e Ch. 8 §8.6.1 (p. 307)
> Similar: Higgins 13e Ch. 8 problems (enterprise vs. equity perspective)

Q: Income tax on the income statement exceeds the tax actually paid this year, and the difference is booked as an increase in deferred tax liabilities. How should a free cash flow built on $EBIT(1-\tau)$ be adjusted?
- Add the increase in deferred taxes back: the formula charges the booked tax, but only the cash tax leaves the firm, so the deferral is a non-cash item.
- Subtract the increase in deferred taxes: it is a liability, and the working-capital term in the formula requires deducting every increase in liabilities.
- Make no adjustment: deferred taxes will be paid at some point, so charging the full booked tax today is the conservative and therefore correct choice.
- Set the tax rate to zero: deferred taxes show the effective rate is temporarily nil, so EBIT should enter the formula untaxed until the tax is paid.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Booked tax = cash tax + increase in deferred taxes. The FCF formula should use cash taxes, so the increase in deferred taxes is added back, just as the operating section of the cash-flow statement does. Working capital subtracts increases in current assets net of non-interest-bearing liabilities, so a rising liability adds to cash.
> Ref: Topic 1 slides 54, 60; Topic 2A slides 24–25
> Similar: PS1 Ex. 1(b)
