## gain

Q: An acquirer values a target by discounted cash flow and must decide how large a control premium it can justify paying. Which pair of statements is correct?
- Discount the target's free cash flows at the acquirer's cost of capital, since the acquirer supplies the funds, and pay a larger premium for a well-run target, since its cash flows are more reliable.
- Discount at the acquirer's cost of capital, since the acquirer's shareholders bear the risk, and cap the premium at the target's liquidation value, since that is what the seller can obtain elsewhere.
- Discount at the target's cost of capital, since the target's risk is what is priced, and pay a larger premium for a well-run target, since its managers will deliver the cash flows in the forecast.
- Discount at a rate set by the risk of the target's own cash flows, and cap the premium at the value of the changes the acquirer can make once in control, which is larger for a poorly run target.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> The discount rate belongs to the cash flows being valued, not to whoever pays for them, so the target's risk (and its sustainable financing) sets it. A control premium is worth paying only for what control lets the buyer change: better management, a different financing policy, synergies. A badly run firm offers more of that, while a well-run firm is already priced near its potential. Liquidation value is a floor only when the holders can force liquidation, and it does not cap the premium.
> Ref: Higgins Ch. 9 §9.2 (p. 341), §9.4.1 (pp. 354–357), §9.1.3 (p. 340)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 1 (Higgins 9.1, items b and c); Lista R9, Ex. 6 (Higgins 9.3)

Q: An acquirer whose shares trade at a high price-to-earnings ratio buys, in a share exchange and with no synergies, a target with a lower ratio, and reports higher earnings per share afterwards. Why is "avoid EPS dilution" a poor merger criterion?
- Because EPS accretion signals value creation only when the target's P/E exceeds the acquirer's, so the criterion is right in principle but is being applied the wrong way round in this deal.
- Because accretion follows mechanically from buying lower-P/E earnings: it lets acquirers overpay for slow-growth targets and rejects high-growth ones; with no synergies, no value is created.
- Because EPS ignores the tax shield on the debt used in a deal, so a share exchange always looks worse than a cash deal, and the criterion biases acquirers toward debt-financed purchases.
- Because accretion transfers value from the target's shareholders to the acquirer's, so the criterion is valid for the acquirer but cannot tell whether the merger is good for the economy.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Issuing high-P/E shares for low-P/E earnings raises combined EPS whether or not any value is created. The market should then give the combined firm a lower P/E, and price per share does not move. The criterion lets the acquirer pay a large premium for a declining, low-P/E firm while ruling out any premium for a high-growth target, which is the reverse of what valuation says. Value comes from synergies and from the price paid relative to the target's value, not from accounting EPS.
> Ref: Higgins Ch. 9 §9.4.2 (pp. 357–363, EPS dilution p. 362)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 7 (Higgins 9.9)

Q: Event studies show target prices jumping on the announcement of a bid while acquirers' returns average close to zero; in some deals the target trades above the offer price right after the announcement. Which reading is most defensible?
- A price above the bid prices in the chance of a higher revised or rival bid; near-zero acquirer returns fit competitive bidding that passes gains to targets, or overpayment offsetting synergies.
- A price above the bid proves the market is inefficient, since no one should pay more than the offer; near-zero acquirer returns show mergers create no value, since the target's gain is a pure transfer.
- A price above the bid reflects a delayed reaction that traders can exploit by buying after the announcement; near-zero acquirer returns show that bidders' shareholders ignore takeover news.
- A price above the bid reflects arbitrageurs short selling the acquirer, which pushes the target up mechanically; near-zero acquirer returns show the deal was fully anticipated before announcement.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> In an efficient market the target's price is the expected payoff across outcomes, so if a competing or improved bid is likely, the price can exceed the current offer. Combined returns are on average positive, but competition among bidders and the free-rider problem leave most of the gain with target holders. Acquirers' near-zero returns are also consistent with hubris or agency-driven overpayment. Price drift before the announcement reflects leaks and rumours. The fast jump at day 0 is evidence of efficiency, not against it.
> Ref: Higgins Ch. 5 §5.3 (pp. 166–169); Higgins Ch. 9 §9.4.3 (p. 364); Amaro de Matos Ch. 8 §8.2.5 (p. 218)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 3 (Higgins 5.10); Lista R9, Ex. 1 (Higgins 9.1, item a)

## free

Q: A raider can raise the value of a widely held firm from $v$ to $\hat v$, normalised so that $\hat v - v = 1$, and makes an unconditional tender offer at price $v + P$; each of a continuum of shareholders takes the success probability as given. Why does the raider earn nothing on the value it creates?
- Because holders hold out for the full $\hat v$ only when the offer is conditional, so an unconditional offer at $v + P$ with $P$ just above zero would succeed and leave most of the gain to the raider.
- Because rival raiders bid the price up to $\hat v$, so the gain is competed away; with one raider facing the same atomistic holders, a premium just above zero would succeed and leave it a profit.
- Because no holder is pivotal, each tenders only if $v + P$ is at least the expected value of keeping the share, so the success probability equals $P$ and the bid hands the whole gain to holders.
- Because holders are irrational and overvalue their shares, demanding more than $\hat v$; with rational holders the offer at $v + P$ would succeed for any $P > 0$ and leave the raider a profit.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> A holder who keeps his share gets $\beta\hat v + (1 - \beta)v$, and one who tenders gets $v + P$. In equilibrium the two must be equal, so $\beta = P$, and the raider's profit $\kappa(\beta - P)$ is zero. The key assumption is that no shareholder is pivotal: each one free-rides on the improvement because his choice does not affect the outcome. Making the offer conditional does not change this. Private benefits, toeholds or dilution are what restore a profit.
> Ref: Tirole Ch. 11 §11.5.1 (pp. 431–432); Amaro de Matos Ch. 8 §8.1.1 (pp. 193–194)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 5 (Amaro de Matos 8.1.5 #1)

Q: A raider who already owns a fraction $\theta$ of the shares needs $\kappa > \theta$ for control, adds value $\hat v - v = 1$, has no private benefit, and offers premium $P \in [0, 1]$ to atomistic holders, so that the success probability equals $P$. What is the raider's profit, and what is its optimal bid?
- $\pi = (\kappa - \theta)(1 - P)$, maximised at $P = 0$: the raider bids no premium, since each unit of premium is paid on every share it buys, so the toehold only lowers the cost.
- $\pi = \kappa P - \theta$, maximised at $P = 1$: the raider bids the full gain and earns $\kappa - \theta$, since the toehold is a sunk cost and the profit comes from the shares bought in the offer.
- $\pi = 0$ for every $P$: the shares bought in the offer earn nothing because of free riding, and the toehold earns nothing too, since its holders also free-ride on the raider's improvement.
- $\pi = \theta P$, maximised at $P = 1$: the shares bought in the offer only break even, while the toehold gains the value created, so the raider bids the full gain and earns $\theta$.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> $\pi(P) = (\kappa - \theta)(\beta - P) + \theta\beta$ with $\beta = P$, so $\pi = \theta P$. The shares bought in the offer are still subject to free riding and earn zero, while the shares already held gain $\beta$. A higher bid raises the success probability and so the value of the toehold, and the raider bids $P = 1$. This is why toeholds make value-raising takeovers possible. A larger toehold is also a signal of a high-value bidder, as in Jegadeesh–Chowdhry.
> Ref: Tirole Ch. 11 §11.5.2 (pp. 432–434); Amaro de Matos Ch. 8 §8.1.4 (pp. 201–204)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 5 (Amaro de Matos 8.1.5 #1); Lista R9, Ex. 10 (Amaro de Matos 8.1.5 #8)

Q: A flip-over poison pill lets the target's remaining shareholders buy new shares cheaply after a hostile takeover, diluting the raider by $\Delta$. The raider enjoys a private benefit of control $\hat w$. In what sense does the pill act like a monopoly price?
- The dilution works as a reserve price: it shifts the raider's benefit to target holders, but if $\hat w$ is uncertain a higher $\Delta$ also blocks raiders with $\hat w < \Delta$, trading rent for deals.
- The pill lets incumbent managers set the price the raider pays, so it always deters takeovers that would raise value, and its only effect is to shield managers' private benefits from control.
- The pill raises the raider's profit, since dilution falls on the holders who did not tender, so the raider can bid less; it is like a monopoly price because the raider becomes the only buyer.
- The dilution is a pure transfer that never changes whether a takeover happens, since any raider with $\hat w > 0$ still bids, so the pill leaves the takeover probability unchanged and only lifts the premium.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Holders are indifferent when $\beta(\hat v + \Delta) + (1 - \beta)v = v + P$, and the raider earns $\beta(\hat w - \Delta)$. It bids $P = 1 + \Delta$ only if $\hat w > \Delta$. With $\hat w$ known, $\Delta^* = \hat w$ extracts the whole control benefit. With $\hat w$ unknown, raising $\Delta$ is like a monopolist raising its price: it gets more from high-$\hat w$ raiders and loses the deals with low-$\hat w$ ones. The pill dilutes the raider, not the holders who keep their shares. Whether managers abuse it is a separate question.
> Ref: Tirole Ch. 11 §11.3 (p. 426), §11.5.2 (pp. 432–434); Tirole Ch. 1 §1.5 (p. 43)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 11 (Tirole 11.1)

## pay

Q: An acquirer whose own value $x$ is known to everyone bids for a target whose stand-alone value $v$ only the target's managers know; the target is worth $w(v) \ge v$ to the acquirer. Why can an offer of a fraction $\beta$ of the merged firm trade where every cash offer fails?
- Because the acquirer's own shares are overvalued, so paying in stock transfers wealth from the target's holders, who accept only because they cannot observe $x$ and so misprice the offer.
- Because the stock payment $\beta[x + w(v)]$ rises with $v$, so it moves with the target's private information; high-value targets that reject cash can accept, and low-value ones are paid less.
- Because a cash offer $C$ reveals the acquirer's valuation, which the target exploits to demand more, while a stock offer keeps that valuation hidden and so stops the target from holding out.
- Because stock offers are accepted whenever $\beta x \ge v$, whatever $w(v)$ is, so the acquirer can pick $\beta$ to buy only the targets whose stand-alone value is below its own firm's value.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> A cash offer $C$ is accepted only by targets with $v \le C$, a lemons problem, and it can fail entirely when $C > E(w \mid C)$. A stock offer pays the target a claim whose value rises with the target's own value, so it partly insures the acquirer against adverse selection. For any cash offer, the stock offer $\beta^* = C/[x + w(C)]$ is accepted by the same targets and leaves the acquirer more wealth. The overvalued-acquirer story is a different model, in which the acquirer has the private information, and here $x$ is common knowledge.
> Ref: Amaro de Matos Ch. 8 §8.2.2 (pp. 207–210)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 8 (Amaro de Matos 8.2.6 #3); Lista R9, Ex. 12 (Amaro de Matos 8.2.6 #4)

Q: A first bidder privately knows its valuation of a target and wants to deter a second bidder from paying to learn its own. The first bidder can offer cash, or a security whose value is high in a good state and low in a bad one. Why do high-valuation first bidders preempt with cash?
- Because cash is cheaper to raise than securities, so only bidders with ample liquidity can offer it; liquidity is correlated with valuation, so a cash bid reveals a high valuation as a by-product.
- Because a security offer reveals the bidder's valuation to the target through its face value, while cash hides it, so a cash bid keeps the second bidder uninformed and unwilling to investigate.
- Because cash costs the same in both states while a security is cheap when the bidder's own valuation is low; so cash is cheaper for high-valuation bidders and signals at a lower face value.
- Because a cash bid commits the bidder to pay even if it loses the contest, which only a high-valuation bidder will accept, so the second bidder infers a high valuation from that commitment alone.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> The gain from offering a security instead of cash is $(1 - \gamma)(p' - v_1^-)$, which falls with the bidder's valuation. A security saves the most for low-valuation bidders, because it lets the target reject in the bad state. Cash is therefore a costly signal that low types will not mimic. Preempting requires the second bidder to infer a valuation above the threshold, and with cash that takes a lower face value than with debt. A bid that is not fully revealing pools all types above the threshold.
> Ref: Amaro de Matos Ch. 8 §8.2.1 (pp. 206–207), §8.2.3 (pp. 212–214)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 9 (Amaro de Matos 8.2.6 #1); Lista R9, Ex. 13 (Amaro de Matos 8.2.6 #8)

## lbo

Q: Leveraged buyouts cluster in mature industries with stable cash flows and unspecialised products, and many management buyouts keep the same top managers yet report better operating results. Which explanation fits both facts?
- Stable cash flows carry heavy debt service and generic assets keep distress costs low; the gains come from managers' larger equity stakes and debt that pre-commits free cash flow, not from new managers.
- Stable cash flows make the firm easy to value, so buyers pay no premium; the operating gains come from wealth taken from the pre-buyout bondholders, which shows up as better results under the same managers.
- Mature firms have the most growth options, which debt preserves; the operating gains come from the tax shield alone, since managers and their incentives are unchanged, so the buyout changes only the tax bill.
- Generic products make the firm easy to resell if the buyout fails; the gains come from replacing the board with the buyout sponsor, so keeping the managers shows that managerial incentives play no role.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Heavy leverage is sustainable only with predictable cash flow and assets that keep their value in distress. Both come from mature, unspecialised businesses with few growth options, which debt overhang would harm. Managers' ownership roughly triples, and debt removes free cash flow from their discretion, so the same people face different incentives. Prebuyout holders capture the tax benefits through the premium, operating gains follow the deal, and bondholder losses are too small to explain the premiums.
> Ref: Amaro de Matos Ch. 7 §7.2.3 (pp. 186–189); Tirole Ch. 1 §1.5 (p. 43); Higgins Ch. 9 §9.4.2 (pp. 357–363)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 2 (Grinblatt & Titman 20.5 and 20.8); Lista R9, Ex. 4 (Amaro de Matos 7.2.4 #3)

Q: Only rival firms in an industry can run a distressed firm's specialised assets, and a buyer must leave the manager of the acquired assets an agency rent to keep her behaving. Per unit, the assets are worth $\rho_1 = p_H R$ in use, and the buyer can pledge only $\rho_0 = p_H(R - B/\Delta p) < 1$, where 1 is the unit investment cost. Which statement about resale prices and borrowing capacity is right?
- The resale price equals $\rho_1$, the value in use, since competition among industry buyers bids it up, so distress sales carry no discount and correlated shocks leave borrowing capacity unchanged.
- The resale price is set by outside buyers' valuation, below $\rho_0$, since industry rivals stay out of distress sales; correlated shocks raise borrowing capacity, since assets become scarce and dearer.
- The resale price equals $\rho_0$ only if the buyer has all the bargaining power; correlated shocks matter only for the buyer's own profits and leave the seller's borrowing capacity unchanged.
- The resale price is at most $\rho_0$, the buyer's pledgeable income, so assets sell below their value in use; correlated shocks mean buyers are distressed when sellers are, which cuts borrowing capacity.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> The buyer's investors can raise only the pledgeable income on the acquired assets, so even a seller with full bargaining power gets $P = \rho_0 < \rho_1$. With less bargaining power, $P$ lies anywhere in $[0, \rho_0]$. The gap $\rho_1 - \rho_0$ is the agency rent the acquiring manager keeps. Expected resale proceeds $(1 - x)(1 - \nu)\rho_0$ enter the debt multiplier $k = 1/\{1 - \rho_0[x + (1 - x)(1 - \nu)]\}$, so a higher correlation $\nu$ of distress lowers borrowing capacity. Specialised assets are less redeployable exactly when they need to be sold.
> Ref: Tirole Ch. 14 §14.2.2 (pp. 500–503); Tirole, Answers to Selected Exercises, Ex. 14.2 (p. 618)
> Similar: Lista R9, Ex. 14 (Tirole 14.2); Lista R9, Ex. 15 (Tirole 14.3)
