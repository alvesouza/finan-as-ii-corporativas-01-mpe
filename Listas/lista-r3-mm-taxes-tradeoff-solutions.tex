\section{Two-outcome project financed with equity or safe debt --- Berk 16.1, p.~490}

\asked
(a) The NPV of the project: what it is worth today minus what it costs.
(b) How much cash the entrepreneur raises by selling the whole project to equity investors, i.e.\
the market value today of an all-equity claim on the project's cash flows.
(c) If the \$100{,}000 is borrowed at the risk-free rate instead, what the shareholders receive
in each state next year, and what that levered equity is worth today according to MM.

\approach
Part (a) is a plain NPV: discount the expected cash flow at the project's cost of capital
(20\%), which prices its risk. Part (b) uses the law of one price: unlevered equity receives
exactly the project's cash flows, so it is worth exactly the project's PV. Part (c) is
\emph{MM Proposition I} in a perfect market (the section heading rules out taxes and distress
costs): debt plus levered equity are claims to the same cash flows, so $E_L+D=V_U$. A last
step checks the answer with \emph{MM Proposition II}, which says how much more the riskier
levered equity must earn.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $C_{\text{low}}=130{,}000$, $C_{\text{high}}=180{,}000$: project free cash flow at $t=1$ in the two states; $p=\tfrac12$: probability of the low state.
  \item $I=100{,}000$: initial investment; $r_U=0.20$: project (unlevered) cost of capital; $r_f=0.10$: risk-free rate.
  \item $V_U$: value of the project's cash flows (= unlevered equity $E_U$); $\mathit{NPV}=V_U-I$.
  \item $D=100{,}000$: amount borrowed at $r_f$; $E_L$: value of levered equity; $r_E$: its expected return; $r_D=r_f$.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{Expected cash flow} The cost of capital is applied to the expected cash flow, so first
average the two equally likely outcomes:
\begin{align}
  \E[C] &= p\,C_{\text{low}}+(1-p)\,C_{\text{high}} \label{eq:1EC}\\
        &= (0.5)(130{,}000)+(0.5)(180{,}000) \nonumber\\
        &= 65{,}000+90{,}000 \nonumber\\
        &= 155{,}000. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{2}{(a) Present value and NPV} Discount \eqref{eq:1EC} one year at the project's cost of
capital and subtract the investment:
\begin{align}
  V_U &= \frac{\E[C]}{1+r_U} \label{eq:1VU}\\
      &= \frac{(155{,}000)}{(1.20)} \nonumber\\
      &= 129{,}166.67, \nonumber\\
  \mathit{NPV} &= V_U - I \label{eq:1NPV}\\
      &= (129{,}166.67)-(100{,}000) \nonumber\\
      &= 29{,}166.67. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(a)} $\mathit{NPV}=\$29{,}166.67$.

\step{3}{(b) Unlevered equity} All-equity holders receive exactly the project's cash flow in
each state, so by the law of one price their claim is worth the PV in \eqref{eq:1VU}:
\begin{align}
  E_U = V_U = 129{,}166.67. \nonumber
\end{align}
Selling the equity raises \$129{,}166.67, which exceeds $I$ by the NPV: the entrepreneur keeps
\$29{,}166.67 after paying for the investment.
\answer{(b)} The unlevered equity is worth (and raises) \$129{,}166.67.

\step{4}{(c) Repayment and the levered equity's cash flows} The loan is repaid with interest
in one year:
\begin{align}
  D(1+r_f) &= (100{,}000)(1.10) \nonumber\\
           &= 110{,}000. \label{eq:1rep}
\end{align}
Since $110{,}000<C_{\text{low}}=130{,}000$, the firm repays in both states: the debt is indeed
riskless, which justifies borrowing at $r_f$. Equity receives what is left:
\begin{align}
  \text{low state:}\quad C_{\text{low}}-110{,}000 &= 130{,}000-110{,}000 = 20{,}000, \nonumber\\
  \text{high state:}\quad C_{\text{high}}-110{,}000 &= 180{,}000-110{,}000 = 70{,}000. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{5}{(c) Value by MM I} With no taxes or distress costs, MM I says the debt and the levered
equity together are worth the unlevered firm, $E_L+D=V_U$. Solving for $E_L$:
\begin{align}
  E_L &= V_U - D \label{eq:1EL}\\
      &= (129{,}166.67)-(100{,}000) \nonumber\\
      &= 29{,}166.67. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{6}{Consistency with MM II} The expected equity cash flow is
$(0.5)(20{,}000)+(0.5)(70{,}000)=10{,}000+35{,}000=45{,}000$, so the expected return implied by
\eqref{eq:1EL} is
\begin{align}
  r_E &= \frac{45{,}000}{E_L}-1 \label{eq:1rE}\\
      &= \frac{45{,}000}{(29{,}166.67)}-1 \nonumber\\
      &= 0.542857. \nonumber
\end{align}
MM II without taxes, $r_E=r_U+\tfrac{D}{E_L}(r_U-r_D)$, needs the debt-equity ratio
\begin{align}
  \frac{D}{E_L} &= \frac{(100{,}000)}{(29{,}166.67)} = 3.428571, \nonumber\\
  r_E &= (0.20)+(3.428571)(0.20-0.10) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.20)+(3.428571)(0.10) \nonumber\\
      &= 0.542857, \nonumber
\end{align}
the same as \eqref{eq:1rE}.
\answer{(c)} Levered equity pays \$20{,}000 (low) or \$70{,}000 (high); by MM it is worth
\$29{,}166.67 (expected return 54.29\%).

\intuition Borrowing slices the same cash flows differently; it does not change their total
value. The entrepreneur captures the same NPV, \$29{,}166.67, either as the excess of the
equity sale over $I$ or as the value of the levered equity left after the loan. The levered
equity bears all the project's risk on a smaller base, so it must offer a higher expected
return (54.29\% against 20\%).

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Discounting the levered equity's expected cash flow at the project's 20\%:
        $45{,}000/1.20=37{,}500$ overvalues it, because levered equity is riskier than the project.
  \item Subtracting only the principal (\$100{,}000) from the cash flows and forgetting the
        \$10{,}000 of interest.
  \item Not checking that the debt is riskless: the risk-free rate is the right borrowing rate
        only because \$110{,}000 is below the worst cash flow.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the book.
\refbib{Berk Ch.~16, §16.2 ``Capital Structure in Perfect Capital Markets'', pp.~464--472 (MM I, MM II).}

\section{One-time dividend or repurchase in a perfect market --- Berk 17.7, p.~528}

\asked
(a) The share price right after the \$250 million is paid out as a dividend.
(b) The share price after the same cash is used to buy back shares instead.
(c) Which of the two policies leaves investors richer in a perfect capital market.

\approach
In a perfect market the share price equals the firm's market value per share, and that value
includes the excess cash. Paying the cash out shrinks the firm by exactly the cash paid. With a
dividend the number of shares is fixed, so the price falls; with a repurchase at a fair price
the number of shares falls in proportion, so the price is unchanged. This is \emph{MM payout
irrelevance}: compare total wealth per share (stock plus cash), not prices.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $N=500$ million shares; $P_0=15$: cum-payout share price; $C=250$ million: excess cash paid out.
  \item $d$: dividend per share; $P_{\text{ex}}$: ex-dividend price; $n_R$: shares repurchased (millions); $P_R$: post-repurchase price.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{Market value of the firm} With no debt, the firm's value is its equity, cash included:
\begin{align}
  N P_0 &= (500)(15) = 7{,}500 \text{ million}, \nonumber\\
  \text{of which operating assets} &= 7{,}500-250 = 7{,}250 \text{ million}. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{2}{(a) Ex-dividend price} The dividend per share is the cash divided by the shares; after
it is paid, the firm holds only the operating assets:
\begin{align}
  d &= \frac{C}{N}=\frac{(250)}{(500)}=0.50, \label{eq:2d}\\
  P_{\text{ex}} &= \frac{N P_0 - C}{N} \label{eq:2Pex}\\
     &= \frac{(7{,}500)-(250)}{(500)} \nonumber\\
     &= \frac{7{,}250}{500} \nonumber\\
     &= 14.50. \nonumber
\end{align}
The price falls by exactly the dividend: $15-0.50=14.50$.
\answer{(a)} $P_{\text{ex}}=\$14.50$.

\step{3}{(b) Repurchase} In a perfect market the shares are bought at the fair cum-payout price
$P_0$, so the cash buys $C/P_0$ shares and the operating assets are spread over the remaining
shares:
\begin{align}
  n_R &= \frac{C}{P_0}=\frac{(250)}{(15)}=16.6667, \label{eq:2nR}\\
  P_R &= \frac{N P_0 - C}{N-n_R} \label{eq:2PR}\\
      &= \frac{(7{,}500)-(250)}{(500)-(16.6667)} \nonumber\\
      &= \frac{7{,}250}{483.3333} \nonumber\\
      &= 15.00. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(b)} $P_R=\$15.00$ (16.67 million shares repurchased).

\step{4}{(c) Comparison of wealth} Per original share, the dividend leaves stock plus cash of
\begin{align}
  P_{\text{ex}}+d &= (14.50)+(0.50) = 15.00, \nonumber
\end{align}
by \eqref{eq:2d}--\eqref{eq:2Pex}; the repurchase leaves either a share worth 15.00 by
\eqref{eq:2PR} or 15.00 in cash for a tendered share. Wealth is \$15 per share under both.

\step{5}{Homemade dividend} An investor with 1{,}000 shares who prefers the dividend but gets the
repurchase sells $500/15=33.33$ shares at \$15 to raise the \$500 the dividend would have paid,
and keeps $1{,}000-33.33=966.67$ shares worth $(1{,}000-500/15)(15)=15{,}000-500=14{,}500$: exactly the dividend
outcome, \$14{,}500 in stock plus \$500 in cash.
\answer{(c)} Neither: wealth is \$15 per share either way, and each investor can convert one
policy into the other at no cost.

\intuition Paying out cash shrinks the firm by exactly the cash paid. With a dividend the price
falls by the dividend; with a repurchase the share count falls instead and the price stays put.
Any investor can create the other pattern for herself (sell shares, or reinvest the dividend),
so payout policy is irrelevant (MM).

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Thinking the repurchase raises the price because there are fewer shares, forgetting that
        the \$250 million of cash leaves the firm too.
  \item Leaving the ex-dividend price at \$15: the share no longer carries the right to the cash.
  \item Comparing prices instead of wealth: \$14.50 $<$ \$15 does not mean the dividend makes
        investors poorer, since they also hold \$0.50 of cash.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the book.
\refbib{Berk Ch.~17, §17.2 ``Dividends Versus Share Repurchases in a Perfect Capital Market'', pp.~503--508.}

\section{MM I with corporate taxes --- Ross 16.14, p.~578}

\asked
The value of Frederico \& Co.\ as it is (all equity), and its value if it borrows \$60{,}000
permanently and uses the money to buy back shares.

\approach
This is \emph{MM Proposition I with corporate taxes}. The all-equity value is the after-tax
perpetual cash flow capitalised at the unlevered cost of capital $R_U$. Perpetual debt creates a
perpetual \emph{interest tax shield} $T_C R_D D$; it is as risky as the debt, so it is discounted
at $R_D$ and is worth $T_C D$. The levered value is $V_L=V_U+T_C D$; the buyback only changes
who holds the claims.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathit{EBIT}=92{,}000$ per year forever; $R_U=0.15$: unlevered cost of equity; $R_D=0.09$: borrowing rate.
  \item $T_C=0.35$: corporate tax rate; $D=60{,}000$: perpetual debt used to buy back shares.
  \item $V_U$, $V_L$: unlevered and levered firm values; $E$: levered equity value; $\mathit{PV}(\text{TS})$: value of the tax shield.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{After-tax cash flow of the all-equity firm} With no interest, the whole EBIT is taxed:
\begin{align}
  \mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C) &= (92{,}000)(1-0.35) \nonumber\\
     &= (92{,}000)(0.65) \nonumber\\
     &= 59{,}800 \text{ per year forever}. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{2}{Unlevered value} A level perpetuity is worth its cash flow divided by its discount rate:
\begin{align}
  V_U &= \frac{\mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)}{R_U} \label{eq:3VU}\\
      &= \frac{(59{,}800)}{(0.15)} \nonumber\\
      &= 398{,}666.67. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{3}{Value of the tax shield} The yearly interest is $R_D D=(0.09)(60{,}000)=5{,}400$, and
deducting it saves $T_C$ times that in tax:
\begin{align}
  T_C R_D D &= (0.35)(5{,}400) = 1{,}890 \text{ per year}. \nonumber
\end{align}
This perpetuity is as risky as the debt, hence discounted at $R_D$; the $R_D$ cancels:
\begin{align}
  \mathit{PV}(\text{TS}) &= \frac{T_C R_D D}{R_D} \label{eq:3TS}\\
      &= T_C D \nonumber\\
      &= \frac{(1{,}890)}{(0.09)} = (0.35)(60{,}000) \nonumber\\
      &= 21{,}000. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{4}{Levered value (MM I with taxes)} Adding \eqref{eq:3TS} to \eqref{eq:3VU},
\begin{align}
  V_L &= V_U + T_C D \label{eq:3VL}\\
      &= (398{,}666.67)+(21{,}000) \nonumber\\
      &= 419{,}666.67, \nonumber\\
  E   &= V_L - D \nonumber\\
      &= (419{,}666.67)-(60{,}000) \nonumber\\
      &= 359{,}666.67. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(firm values)} Unlevered: $V_U=\$398{,}666.67$. With \$60{,}000 of debt: $V_L=\$419{,}666.67$
(debt \$60{,}000, equity \$359{,}666.67).

\intuition The government's claim on EBIT falls by \$1{,}890 a year once interest is
deductible; that perpetual saving, worth $T_C D$, accrues to the firm's investors. The
borrowing rate does not affect the answer because it scales both the saving and its discount
rate.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Capitalising pre-tax EBIT ($92{,}000/0.15$) instead of the after-tax cash flow.
  \item Discounting the tax shield at $R_U$: $1{,}890/0.15=12{,}600$ understates it; with fixed
        perpetual debt the shield is as safe as the debt.
  \item Adding the whole \$60{,}000 to firm value: the debt replaces equity; only $T_C D$ is new value.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the local copy (selected answers are in the web-only Apêndice C).
\refbib{Ross Cap.~16, §16.4 ``Proposições I e II de M\&M com impostos sobre o lucro da pessoa jurídica'', pp.~551--556.}

\section{APV with constant debt --- Higgins 8.23, p.~333}

\asked
The adjusted present value of Gila's project: its base-case NPV plus the value of the interest
tax shields from \$1 million of debt held for three years.

\approach
\emph{APV} values the project as if all-equity financed (given: \$500{,}000) and then adds the
PV of each financing side effect; here the only one is the \emph{interest tax shield}. The debt
is a fixed amount for three years, so the shield is a three-year annuity, and the statement says
to discount it at the 8\% debt rate.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathit{NPV}_0=500{,}000$: base-case NPV of the project's free cash flows.
  \item $D=1{,}000{,}000$: constant debt for $T=3$ years; $r=0.08$: interest and discount rate of the shields; $t=0.40$: tax rate.
  \item $\mathit{TS}$: annual interest tax shield, received at the end of years 1--3; $A$: annuity factor; $\mathit{APV}$: adjusted present value.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{Annual shield} Interest is charged on the constant balance, and the shield is the tax
rate times the interest:
\begin{align}
  \text{interest} &= D\,r = (1{,}000{,}000)(0.08) = 80{,}000, \nonumber\\
  \mathit{TS} &= t \cdot D\,r \label{eq:4TS}\\
       &= (0.40)(80{,}000) \nonumber\\
       &= 32{,}000. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{2}{Three-year annuity factor at 8\%} Discount each year's \$1 and add:
\begin{align}
  A &= \frac{1}{1.08}+\frac{1}{1.08^2}+\frac{1}{1.08^3} \label{eq:4A}\\
    &= (0.925926)+(0.857339)+(0.793832) \nonumber\\
    &= 2.577097. \nonumber
\end{align}
The closed form gives the same: $A=\bigl[1-1/1.08^3\bigr]/0.08=\bigl[1-1/1.259712\bigr]/0.08=2.577097$.

\step{3}{PV of the shields and APV} Multiply the level shield \eqref{eq:4TS} by \eqref{eq:4A}
and add the base case:
\begin{align}
  \mathit{PV}(\text{TS}) &= \mathit{TS}\cdot A \label{eq:4PV}\\
      &= (32{,}000)(2.577097) \nonumber\\
      &= 82{,}467.10, \nonumber\\
  \mathit{APV} &= \mathit{NPV}_0+\mathit{PV}(\text{TS}) \label{eq:4APV}\\
     &= (500{,}000)+(82{,}467.10) \nonumber\\
     &= 582{,}467.10. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(APV)} $\mathit{APV}=\$582{,}467.10$.

\intuition APV separates the value of the operations (\$500{,}000) from the value created by the
financing side effect. With a fixed debt the shields are as safe as the debt, so they are
discounted at the debt rate.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Treating the shield as perpetual ($tD=400{,}000$, APV \$900{,}000): the debt lives only three years.
  \item Adding the interest (\$80{,}000 a year) instead of the tax saved on it (\$32{,}000).
  \item Discounting the shields at the cost of equity, which the statement rules out.
\end{itemize}

\bookans Matches the book's answer, \$582{,}467 (p.~425).
\refbib{Higgins Ch.~8, Appendix ``Asset Beta and Adjusted Present Value'', pp.~318--325 (APV, p.~321).}

\section{MM II with a rising cost of debt --- Berk 16.5, p.~491}

\asked
(a) Hardmon's expected equity return if it levers to $D/E=0.50$ and pays 6\% on debt.
(b) The same with $D/E=1.50$ and an 8\% debt rate.
(c) Whether shareholders are best served by the capital structure with the highest expected
stock return.

\approach
With no taxes or distress costs (section heading), \emph{MM I} fixes the firm's total value and
\emph{MM II} follows: the expected return on the assets (the WACC) stays at the unlevered 12\%,
so the cost of equity rises with leverage to keep the weighted average at 12\%. We derive MM II
from that average and apply it, letting $r_D$ change with the debt level.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $r_U=0.12$: expected return on unlevered equity (= on the assets, unchanged by leverage without taxes).
  \item $D/E$: debt-equity ratio (0.50 or 1.50); $r_D$: debt cost of capital (0.06 or 0.08); $r_E$: expected return on levered equity; $V=D+E$.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{Derive MM II} The assets' return is the value-weighted average of the returns on the
claims that finance them:
\begin{align}
  r_U &= \frac{E}{D+E}\,r_E+\frac{D}{D+E}\,r_D. \nonumber
\end{align}
Multiply both sides by $D+E$:
\begin{align}
  (D+E)\,r_U &= E\,r_E + D\,r_D. \nonumber
\end{align}
Move $D r_D$ to the left and write $(D+E)r_U=E r_U+D r_U$:
\begin{align}
  E\,r_E &= E\,r_U + D\,r_U - D\,r_D = E\,r_U + D\,(r_U-r_D). \nonumber
\end{align}
Divide by $E>0$:
\begin{align}
  r_E = r_U + \frac{D}{E}\,(r_U-r_D). \label{eq:5MM2}
\end{align}

\step{2}{(a) $D/E=0.50$, $r_D=6\%$} Substituting into \eqref{eq:5MM2},
\begin{align}
  r_E &= (0.12)+(0.50)(0.12-0.06) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.12)+(0.50)(0.06) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.12)+(0.03) \nonumber\\
      &= 0.15. \label{eq:5a}
\end{align}
\answer{(a)} $r_E=15\%$.

\step{3}{(b) $D/E=1.50$, $r_D=8\%$} Substituting into \eqref{eq:5MM2},
\begin{align}
  r_E &= (0.12)+(1.50)(0.12-0.08) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.12)+(1.50)(0.04) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.12)+(0.06) \nonumber\\
      &= 0.18. \label{eq:5b}
\end{align}
\answer{(b)} $r_E=18\%$.

\step{4}{Check: the WACC is unchanged} Convert $D/E$ into weights: $E/V=1/(1+D/E)$ and
$D/V=(D/E)/(1+D/E)$. In (a) $E/V=1/1.5=0.666667$, $D/V=0.5/1.5=0.333333$; in (b)
$E/V=1/2.5=0.4$, $D/V=1.5/2.5=0.6$. Then
\begin{align}
  \text{(a)}\quad & (0.666667)(0.15)+(0.333333)(0.06)=0.10+0.02=0.12, \nonumber\\
  \text{(b)}\quad & (0.4)(0.18)+(0.6)(0.08)=0.072+0.048=0.12. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{5}{(c) Reply to the manager} The higher expected return in \eqref{eq:5b} is pure
compensation for higher risk: equity bears the same business risk on a thinner base. By MM I the
firm's value, and therefore the share price, is the same at every debt level; the WACC stays at
12\% (Step~4). Choosing leverage to maximise $r_E$ does not raise shareholder wealth;
shareholders can also lever up on their own (homemade leverage) if they want a riskier,
higher-return position.
\answer{(c)} Reject the argument: under MM a higher $r_E$ only compensates for higher risk;
firm value and the share price do not change, so shareholders are no better off.

\intuition The cost of equity rises linearly with $D/E$ at slope $r_U-r_D$. When debt becomes
riskier ($r_D$ rises), part of the risk moves to creditors and $r_E$ rises less steeply, but the
total required return on the assets is still $r_U$.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Using $D/V$ instead of $D/E$ in MM II: in (a) that gives $0.12+(0.333333)(0.06)=0.14$.
  \item Keeping $r_D=6\%$ in (b): $0.12+(1.50)(0.06)=0.21$ overstates $r_E$, since riskier
        debt takes on part of the risk.
  \item Equating a higher expected return with a higher value: value depends on cash flows and
        their risk together.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the book.
\refbib{Berk Ch.~16, §16.2, pp.~464--472 (MM II, leverage and the cost of equity).}

\section{Tax shields on amortising debt --- Berk 16.11, p.~492}

\asked
The present value of the interest tax shields generated by a \$100 million loan at 10\% that is
paid down by \$25 million a year, with a 40\% tax rate and shields as risky as the loan.

\approach
The \emph{interest tax shield} each year is $\tau_c$ times that year's interest, and interest is
charged on the balance still outstanding, so the shields shrink as the loan amortises. Because
the debt is not permanent, the shortcut $\tau_c D$ does not apply: build the year-by-year
schedule and discount each shield at the loan rate, as the statement prescribes.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $B_t$: debt outstanding during year $t=1,\dots,4$ (millions); $B_1=100$, $B_{t+1}=B_t-25$.
  \item $r_D=0.10$: interest rate and discount rate of the shields; $\tau_c=0.40$: marginal corporate tax rate.
  \item $\mathit{TS}_t=\tau_c r_D B_t$: tax shield received at the end of year $t$.
\end{itemize}

\assumption The first \$25 million repayment is made one year from now, together with the
first interest payment, and interest in year $t$ is charged on the balance at the start of the
year. The loan is then repaid after four years.

\step{1}{Schedule} Apply the 10\% rate to each start-of-year balance and the 40\% tax rate to
each interest payment:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crrrr}
\toprule
Year $t$ & Balance $B_t$ & Interest $r_D B_t$ & Repayment & Shield $\tau_c r_D B_t$\\
\midrule
1 & 100 & 10.0 & 25 & 4\\
2 &  75 &  7.5 & 25 & 3\\
3 &  50 &  5.0 & 25 & 2\\
4 &  25 &  2.5 & 25 & 1\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{align}
  \mathit{TS}_t &= (0.40)(0.10)B_t = (0.04)B_t \in \{4,\ 3,\ 2,\ 1\}. \label{eq:6TS}
\end{align}

\step{2}{Discount factors at 10\%}
$1/1.10=0.909091$, $1/1.10^2=1/1.21=0.826446$, $1/1.10^3=1/1.331=0.751315$,
$1/1.10^4=1/1.4641=0.683013$.

\step{3}{Present value} Multiply each shield \eqref{eq:6TS} by its factor and add:
\begin{align}
  \mathit{PV}(\text{TS}) &= \sum_{t=1}^{4}\frac{\mathit{TS}_t}{(1+r_D)^t} \label{eq:6PV}\\
      &= \frac{(4)}{1.10}+\frac{(3)}{1.21}+\frac{(2)}{1.331}+\frac{(1)}{1.4641} \nonumber\\
      &= 3.636364+2.479339+1.502630+0.683013 \nonumber\\
      &= 8.301346. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(PV of the shields)} $\mathit{PV}(\text{TS})\approx\$8.30$ million.

\intuition Each year's shield is proportional to the debt still outstanding, so amortisation
shrinks the shield stream. A permanent \$100 million loan would be worth $\tau_c D=\$40$
million in shields; repaying it over four years leaves $8.301346/40=0.2075$, about a fifth of
that.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Using $\tau_c D=\$40$ million, the value for \emph{permanent} debt.
  \item Charging interest on the end-of-year balance (shields 3, 2, 1, 0), which gives
        $3/1.1+2/1.21+1/1.331=5.131480$ and misses the first year's interest.
  \item Adding the shields undiscounted (\$10 million) or discounting them at an unlevered rate.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the book.
\refbib{Berk Ch.~16, §16.3 ``Debt and Taxes'', pp.~473--479 (value of the interest tax shield).}

\section{Homemade leverage between twin firms --- Ross 16.18, p.~578}

\asked
(a) An investor who can borrow at 6\% wants 5\% of Cavaleiro (levered). Can she earn more by
buying 5\% of Weber (unlevered) and borrowing personally, spending the same amount of her own
money? (b) Which strategy investors will choose, and when this process stops.

\approach
This is \emph{homemade leverage}, the arbitrage behind \emph{MM Proposition I} without taxes.
Two firms with the same operating income must have the same total value; if not, investors
replicate one firm's equity with the other firm's equity plus personal borrowing or lending. Here
$V_C=1{,}632{,}000+1{,}200{,}000=2{,}832{,}000>V_W=2{,}500{,}000$, so Cavaleiro is overpriced and
the cheaper route to its payoff goes through Weber.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $X$: perpetual operating income of each firm (expected 400{,}000; random, identical for both); no taxes.
  \item Weber (unlevered): $E_W=V_W=2{,}500{,}000$. Cavaleiro (levered): $E_C=1{,}632{,}000$, $D_C=1{,}200{,}000$, interest $I_C=72{,}000$ per year, so $r_D=72{,}000/1{,}200{,}000=0.06$.
  \item $\alpha=0.05$: fraction bought; $r=0.06$: personal borrowing rate; $L$: amount borrowed personally.
\end{itemize}

\assumption ``Os custos líquidos das estratégias sejam iguais'' is read as: both strategies
require the same own funds today, the \$81{,}600 that 5\% of Cavaleiro costs.

\step{1}{Strategy C: 5\% of Cavaleiro} Cavaleiro's shareholders receive operating income minus
interest, $X-I_C$; with the projected income that is $400{,}000-72{,}000=328{,}000$. Then
\begin{align}
  \text{cost}_C &= \alpha E_C=(0.05)(1{,}632{,}000)=81{,}600, \label{eq:7cC}\\
  \text{income}_C &= \alpha (X-I_C)=(0.05)(328{,}000)=16{,}400, \label{eq:7iC}
\end{align}
a return of $16{,}400/81{,}600=20.10\%$.

\step{2}{Strategy W: 5\% of Weber with the same own funds} The Weber stake costs
$(0.05)(2{,}500{,}000)=125{,}000$ and earns $(0.05)(400{,}000)=20{,}000$. With own funds
\eqref{eq:7cC}, the rest is borrowed at 6\%:
\begin{align}
  L &= 125{,}000-81{,}600 = 43{,}400, \label{eq:7L}\\
  \text{income}_W &= 20{,}000 - rL \nonumber\\
      &= (20{,}000)-(0.06)(43{,}400) \nonumber\\
      &= (20{,}000)-(2{,}604) = 17{,}396, \label{eq:7iW}
\end{align}
a return of $17{,}396/81{,}600=21.32\%$.

\step{3}{(a) Same risk, higher income} Write both incomes for any realisation of $X$:
\begin{align}
  \text{income}_C &= 0.05\,(X-72{,}000) = 0.05X-3{,}600, \nonumber\\
  \text{income}_W &= 0.05X-(0.06)(43{,}400) = 0.05X-2{,}604. \nonumber
\end{align}
Both move one-for-one with $0.05X$, so they carry the same operating risk, and W pays
$3{,}600-2{,}604=996$ more in every state. The W position even uses less leverage: \$43{,}400 of
personal debt against her 5\% share of Cavaleiro's \$1{,}200{,}000, i.e.\ \$60{,}000.
\answer{(a)} Yes: for the same \$81{,}600, 5\% of Weber plus \$43{,}400 borrowed pays \$17{,}396
(21.32\%) against \$16{,}400 (20.10\%), \$996 more in every state at no extra risk.

\step{4}{The underlying arbitrage} Replicating strategy C exactly needs the investor's share of
Cavaleiro's debt, $L=\alpha D_C=(0.05)(1{,}200{,}000)=60{,}000$: income
$20{,}000-(0.06)(60{,}000)=20{,}000-3{,}600=16{,}400$, identical to \eqref{eq:7iC}, for own funds
$125{,}000-60{,}000=65{,}000$. The saving is
\begin{align}
  81{,}600-65{,}000 = 16{,}600 = \alpha (V_C-V_W) = (0.05)(2{,}832{,}000-2{,}500{,}000) = (0.05)(332{,}000), \label{eq:7arb}
\end{align}
and investing it at 6\% yields $(0.06)(16{,}600)=996$ a year, the gap found in Step~3.

\step{5}{(b) What investors do and when it stops} Every Cavaleiro shareholder gains by selling
Cavaleiro shares and buying Weber shares with personal borrowing. Selling pressure lowers $E_C$
and buying pressure raises $E_W$ until the arbitrage profit \eqref{eq:7arb} is zero, i.e.\ until
$V_C=V_W$ (MM I). If Weber's value stays at \$2.5 million, it stops when
$E_C=2{,}500{,}000-1{,}200{,}000=1{,}300{,}000$. Cavaleiro's cost of equity is then
$328{,}000/1{,}300{,}000=25.23\%$, which is MM II with $r_U=400{,}000/2{,}500{,}000=0.16$:
\begin{align}
  r_E &= 0.16+\tfrac{1.2}{1.3}(0.16-0.06) = 0.16+(0.923077)(0.10) = 0.16+0.092308 = 0.252308. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(b)} Investors sell Cavaleiro and buy Weber on personal margin; the process ends when the
two firms have the same total value, $V_C=V_W$ (e.g.\ $E_C=\$1{,}300{,}000$ if $V_W$ stays at
\$2.5 million).

\intuition Investors can borrow on the same terms as the firm, so corporate leverage adds
nothing they cannot build themselves. A levered firm that trades at a premium is selling
something investors can make more cheaply at home, and arbitrage removes the premium.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Comparing the firms' returns on equity without equalising own funds (or risk): Weber's
        unlevered 16\% is not comparable with Cavaleiro's 20.10\%.
  \item Borrowing the full \$60{,}000 and then comparing incomes: that equalises income, not cost;
        the gain then shows up as \$16{,}600 saved today instead of \$996 a year.
  \item Forgetting that the process stops at equal \emph{firm} values, not equal share prices or equal returns.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the local copy (selected answers are in the web-only Apêndice C).
\refbib{Ross Cap.~16, §16.2 ``O efeito da alavancagem financeira'' (homemade leverage), pp.~542--547; §16.3, pp.~547--551 (Propositions I and II).}

\section{Announcement effect of a debt-financed repurchase with distress costs --- Berk 16.22, p.~494}

\asked
(a) The share price after Hawar announces a permanent \$20 million debt-financed buyback, if
corporate taxes are the only imperfection.
(b) If the price actually rises only to \$5.75, the PV of the financial distress costs the market
attributes to the new debt.

\approach
Permanent debt creates an interest tax shield worth $\tau_c D$ (\emph{MM I with taxes}). When the
plan is announced, the whole change in firm value is capitalised into the price of the existing
shares, before any shares are bought back. With distress costs, the \emph{static trade-off}
identity $V_L=V_U+\tau_c D-\mathit{PV}(\text{FDC})$ lets us back out $\mathit{PV}(\text{FDC})$ from
the observed price.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_0=5.50$, $N=10$ million shares, so $E_0=(5.50)(10)=55$ million.
  \item $D=20$ million of permanent new debt; $\tau_c=0.30$; $P_1$: price after the announcement; $\mathit{PV}(\text{FDC})$: PV of financial distress costs.
\end{itemize}

\assumption The announcement is credible and all the value change is priced at once; shares are
later bought back at the post-announcement price, so the repurchase itself transfers no value.

\step{1}{(a) Value of the tax shield} Permanent debt adds (cf.\ \eqref{eq:3TS})
\begin{align}
  \tau_c D = (0.30)(20) = 6 \text{ million}. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{2}{(a) Price after the announcement} The \$6 million goes to the 10 million shares
outstanding at the announcement:
\begin{align}
  P_1 &= \frac{E_0+\tau_c D}{N} \label{eq:8P1}\\
      &= \frac{(55)+(6)}{(10)} \nonumber\\
      &= 6.10. \nonumber
\end{align}
Check that the buyback leaves it there: \$20 million buys $20/6.10=3.278689$ million shares;
equity falls to $61-20=41$ million over $10-3.278689=6.721311$ million shares, i.e.\
$41/6.721311=6.10$.
\answer{(a)} $P_1=\$6.10$ (a gain of \$0.60 per share).

\step{3}{(b) Distress costs implied by \$5.75} The observed gain in equity value is
\begin{align}
  (5.75)(10)-(55) = 57.5-55 = 2.5 \text{ million}. \nonumber
\end{align}
By the trade-off identity this gain equals the tax benefit net of distress costs; solving for
the distress costs:
\begin{align}
  2.5 &= \tau_c D-\mathit{PV}(\text{FDC}) \nonumber\\
  \mathit{PV}(\text{FDC}) &= \tau_c D - 2.5 \nonumber\\
      &= (6)-(2.5) \nonumber\\
      &= 3.5 \text{ million}. \label{eq:8FDC}
\end{align}
(The buyback at \$5.75 again leaves the price unchanged: $20/5.75=3.478261$ million shares,
$37.5/(10-3.478261)=37.5/6.521739=5.75$.)
\answer{(b)} $\mathit{PV}(\text{FDC})=\$3.5$ million.

\intuition The market pays the shareholders for the expected tax savings and charges them for
expected distress costs. The smaller price jump (\$0.25 against \$0.60) shows that the market
expects distress costs to eat more than half of the tax benefit of this debt.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Dividing the gain by the post-repurchase share count: the announcement gain accrues to
        all 10 million shares outstanding when the news arrives.
  \item Looking for an interest rate: with permanent debt the shield is worth $\tau_c D$
        whatever the rate.
  \item Reading \$5.75 as a loss because it is below \$6.10: the \$3.5 million is the gap between
        the two prices times 10 million shares.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the book.
\refbib{Berk Ch.~16, §16.3 pp.~473--479 and §16.5 ``Optimal Capital Structure: The Tradeoff Theory'', pp.~480--482.}

\section{After-tax WACC with riskless perpetual debt, then a debt-for-equity exchange --- Grinblatt \& Titman 13.15--13.16, p.~494}

\asked
13.15: Akron's expected equity return (from the CAPM) and its after-tax WACC with \$50 million
of riskless perpetual debt and a 40\% tax rate.
13.16: Akron's WACC after it retires half its debt by issuing \$25 million of equity to the
debt holders, under the Hamada model's debt policy.

\approach
The CAPM gives $r_E$ from $\beta_E$; G\&T's (13.8) then gives the after-tax WACC. For 13.16 the
\emph{Hamada model} (static perpetual riskless debt) says the firm is unlevered assets plus a
riskless tax shield $T_cD$. The unlevered assets, their beta and their return $r_{UA}$ do not
change with leverage, so we unlever once and then use the \emph{MM adjusted cost of capital}
$\mathit{WACC}=r_{UA}(1-T_cD/V)$ at the new debt level, cross-checking through the relevered
cost of equity.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $D=50$, $E=50$ (millions): perpetual riskless debt and equity; $A=D+E=100$: market value of the assets.
  \item $\beta_E=1.2$; $r_f=0.08$; $\E[r_M]=0.13$; CAPM holds; $r_D=r_f$ (riskless debt); $T_c=0.40$.
  \item $\mathit{UA}$, $\mathit{TX}$: unlevered assets and debt tax shield, $A=\mathit{UA}+\mathit{TX}$; $\beta_{UA}$, $r_{UA}$: their beta and expected return.
  \item Primes ($D'$, $E'$, $V'$, $\mathit{WACC}'$) denote values after the exchange offer.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{13.15: cost of equity (CAPM)} The market risk premium is $0.13-0.08=0.05$, so
\begin{align}
  r_E &= r_f+\beta_E(\E[r_M]-r_f) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.08)+(1.2)(0.13-0.08) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.08)+(1.2)(0.05) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.08)+(0.06) \nonumber\\
      &= 0.14. \label{eq:9rE}
\end{align}

\step{2}{13.15: WACC} Use G\&T's after-tax WACC (13.8) with market weights $50/100=0.5$ each and
\eqref{eq:9rE}:
\begin{align}
  \mathit{WACC} &= \frac{E}{D+E}\,r_E+\frac{D}{D+E}\,r_D(1-T_c) \label{eq:9W}\\
      &= (0.5)(0.14)+(0.5)(0.08)(1-0.40) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.07)+(0.5)(0.08)(0.60) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.07)+(0.024) \nonumber\\
      &= 0.094. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(13.15)} $r_E=14\%$; $\mathit{WACC}=9.40\%$.

\step{3}{Unlevered cost of capital (Hamada)} With static perpetual riskless debt,
$\mathit{TX}=T_cD$ (13.2) and $\mathit{UA}=D+E-T_cD=100-(0.40)(50)=100-20=80$ (13.3). Invert
(13.6), $\beta_E=\bigl[1+(1-T_c)\tfrac{D}{E}\bigr]\beta_{UA}$, by dividing both sides by the
bracket:
\begin{align}
  \beta_{UA} &= \frac{\beta_E}{1+(1-T_c)D/E} \label{eq:9bUA}\\
      &= \frac{(1.2)}{1+(1-0.40)(50/50)} \nonumber\\
      &= \frac{1.2}{1+0.6} = \frac{1.2}{1.6} \nonumber\\
      &= 0.75, \nonumber\\
  r_{UA} &= r_f+\beta_{UA}(\E[r_M]-r_f) \label{eq:9rUA}\\
      &= (0.08)+(0.75)(0.05) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.08)+(0.0375) \nonumber\\
      &= 0.1175. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{4}{The adjusted cost of capital formula} Start from (13.11),
$\mathit{WACC}=\bar r_A-\tfrac{D}{V}T_c r_D$, and substitute the Hamada form of (13.1b),
$\bar r_A=\bigl(1-\tfrac{T_cD}{V}\bigr)r_{UA}+\tfrac{T_cD}{V}r_D$:
\begin{align}
  \mathit{WACC} &= \Bigl(1-\frac{T_cD}{V}\Bigr)r_{UA}+\frac{T_cD}{V}r_D-\frac{D}{V}T_c r_D \nonumber\\
      &= \Bigl(1-\frac{T_cD}{V}\Bigr)r_{UA} \nonumber\\
      &= r_{UA}\Bigl(1-T_c\frac{D}{V}\Bigr), \label{eq:9ACC}
\end{align}
since the two $r_D$ terms are equal and cancel. Check at today's leverage:
$(0.1175)\bigl(1-(0.40)(0.5)\bigr)=(0.1175)(0.8)=0.094$, equal to \eqref{eq:9W}.

\step{5}{13.16: values after retiring \$25 million of debt} The unlevered assets do not change;
the shield shrinks to $T_cD'=(0.40)(25)=10$. Hence
\begin{align}
  V' &= \mathit{UA}+T_cD' = (80)+(10) = 90, \label{eq:9V}\\
  E' &= V'-D' = (90)-(25) = 65, \nonumber
\end{align}
of which \$25 million is the new shares given to the old creditors and
$65-25=50-(0.40)(25)=40$ is the old shareholders' stake: they bear the \$10 million loss of
shield value.

\step{6}{13.16: new WACC} The new debt ratio is $D'/V'=25/90=0.277778$; substitute it into
\eqref{eq:9ACC} with \eqref{eq:9rUA}:
\begin{align}
  \mathit{WACC}' &= r_{UA}\Bigl(1-T_c\frac{D'}{V'}\Bigr) \label{eq:9W2}\\
      &= (0.1175)\bigl(1-(0.40)(0.277778)\bigr) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.1175)(0.888889) \nonumber\\
      &= 0.104444. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{7}{Cross-check through the cost of equity} Relever with (13.6) at $D'/E'=25/65=0.384615$:
\begin{align}
  \beta_E' &= \beta_{UA}\Bigl[1+(1-T_c)\frac{D'}{E'}\Bigr] \nonumber\\
      &= (0.75)\bigl[1+(0.6)(0.384615)\bigr] = (0.75)(1+0.230769) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.75)(1.230769) = 0.923077, \nonumber\\
  r_E' &= (0.08)+(0.923077)(0.05) = (0.08)+(0.046154) = 0.126154, \nonumber\\
  \mathit{WACC}' &= \tfrac{65}{90}(0.126154)+\tfrac{25}{90}(0.08)(0.6) \nonumber\\
      &= (0.091111)+(0.013333) = 0.104444, \nonumber
\end{align}
the same as \eqref{eq:9W2}. The WACC rises from 9.40\% to 10.44\%.
\answer{(13.16)} $\mathit{WACC}'=10.44\%$.

\assumption The debt ratio in \eqref{eq:9W2} is measured at post-exchange market values. Using
$D'/V=25/100$ (ignoring the lost shield) would give $(0.1175)(1-0.40\cdot0.25)=10.575\%$; that
mixes the old firm value with the new debt and is not consistent with the Hamada model.

\intuition In the Hamada model the only thing debt adds is a riskless tax shield. Cutting the debt
removes part of that cheap, government-funded slice of the financing, so the WACC moves back
toward the unlevered cost of capital, 11.75\%.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Computing the pre-tax WACC, $(0.5)(0.14)+(0.5)(0.08)=0.11$, instead of (13.8) with
        $(1-T_c)$ on debt.
  \item Keeping $r_E=14\%$ after the exchange: $\tfrac{65}{90}(0.14)+\tfrac{25}{90}(0.048)=0.114444$
        ignores that less leverage makes equity safer.
  \item Weighting by the old value $V=100$ (10.575\%): retiring debt destroys \$10 million of
        shield, so $V'=90$.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the book.
\refbib{Grinblatt \& Titman Ch.~13: Hamada model, eqs.\ (13.1)--(13.7), pp.~463--466; WACC (13.8), p.~476; adjusted cost of capital formula, pp.~481--482.}

\section{Taxes vs.\ bankruptcy costs in two states --- Grinblatt \& Titman 16.2, p.~591}

\asked
(a) The value of Nigel's firm if all equity financed.
(b) What the firm raises today by selling a zero-coupon bond that promises \$44 next period.
(c) The value of the equity with that bond outstanding.
(d) The total value of the firm with that bond.
(e) Whether the firm would be worth more with a \$70 obligation instead.

\approach
Investors are risk neutral, so every claim is worth its expected payoff discounted at the 10\%
riskless rate. Debt brings a tax saving of \$0.05 per dollar raised; debt large enough to default
in the bad state also triggers a \$20 bankruptcy cost. This is the \emph{static trade-off}: firm
value $=V_U+\mathit{PV}(\text{tax saving})-\mathit{PV}(\text{bankruptcy costs})$. For each debt
level we first check whether default happens, then price debt and equity state by state.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $C_G=210$, $C_B=66$: pre-tax cash flows next period in the favourable and unfavourable state, each with probability $\tfrac12$.
  \item $t=\tfrac13$: tax rate of the all-equity firm; $s=0.05$: tax saved per dollar of debt financing; $r_f=0.10$; investors are risk neutral.
  \item $F$: face value of the zero-coupon debt; $D$: its market value today; $K=20$: bankruptcy cost; $V_U$, $E$, $V$: unlevered value, equity, levered firm value.
\end{itemize}

\assumption ``Debt financing used'' is the amount raised today, $D$; the saving $sD$ is
realised next period in both states (if the firm defaults it accrues to the creditors who take
it over). The answer to (e) does not depend on this reading (Step~7).

\step{1}{(a) All-equity value} After paying one third in tax, the firm keeps two thirds:
$210(1-\tfrac13)=140$ and $66(1-\tfrac13)=44$. Discount the expected value:
\begin{align}
  V_U &= \frac{\tfrac12(140)+\tfrac12(44)}{1.10} \label{eq:10VU}\\
      &= \frac{70+22}{1.10} = \frac{92}{1.10} = \frac{920}{11} \nonumber\\
      &= 83.6364. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(a)} $V_U=\$83.64$.

\step{2}{(b) Debt with $F=44$} Guess that the debt is safe; then it is worth its face
discounted at the riskless rate, and the tax saving follows:
\begin{align}
  D &= \frac{44}{1.10} = 40, \qquad sD=(0.05)(40)=2. \nonumber
\end{align}
Verify the guess: in the bad state the firm has $44+2=46\ge 44$, so it pays in full in both
states.
\answer{(b)} The firm receives $D=\$40$.

\step{3}{(c) Equity} Equity gets what is left after paying 44: $140+2-44=98$ in the good state
and $46-44=2$ in the bad state:
\begin{align}
  E &= \frac{\tfrac12(98)+\tfrac12(2)}{1.10} \label{eq:10E}\\
    &= \frac{49+1}{1.10} = \frac{50}{1.10} = \frac{500}{11} \nonumber\\
    &= 45.4545. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(c)} $E=\$45.45$.

\step{4}{(d) Firm value} Add the two claims:
\begin{align}
  V &= D+E \label{eq:10V}\\
    &= (40)+(45.4545) \nonumber\\
    &= 85.4545, \nonumber
\end{align}
equal to $V_U+\tfrac{2}{1.10}=\tfrac{920}{11}+\tfrac{20}{11}=\tfrac{940}{11}$: the PV of the tax
saving, $1.8182$, is the whole gain.
\answer{(d)} $V=\$85.45$.

\step{5}{(e) Debt with $F=70$: price the risky debt} Now the bad-state cash, $44+sD$, is below
70 for any plausible $D$, so the firm defaults; creditors take over, pay the \$20 cost and
receive $44+0.05D-20=24+0.05D$. In the good state they get 70. The debt's price solves
\begin{align}
  D &= \frac{\tfrac12(70)+\tfrac12(24+0.05D)}{1.10} \label{eq:10De}\\
  1.10\,D &= 35+12+0.025\,D \nonumber\\
  1.10\,D-0.025\,D &= 47 \nonumber\\
  1.075\,D &= 47 \nonumber\\
  D &= \frac{47}{1.075} = 43.72093. \nonumber
\end{align}
Then $sD=(0.05)(43.72093)=2.18605$ and the bad-state recovery is $24+2.18605=26.18605<70$:
default confirmed.

\step{6}{(e) Equity and firm value} Equity gets $140+2.18605-70=72.18605$ in the good state and
0 in the bad state:
\begin{align}
  E &= \frac{\tfrac12(72.18605)+\tfrac12(0)}{1.10} \nonumber\\
    &= 32.81184, \nonumber\\
  V &= D+E \label{eq:10Ve}\\
    &= (43.72093)+(32.81184) \nonumber\\
    &= 76.53277. \nonumber
\end{align}
Equivalently $V=V_U+\mathit{PV}(sD)-\mathit{PV}(K)=(83.63636)+(1.98732)-(9.09091)=76.53277$,
using $2.18605/1.10=1.98732$ and $\tfrac12(20)/1.10=9.09091$.

\step{7}{Robustness} Whatever the basis of the saving, it is at most $0.05\times70$ next period,
worth $3.18182$, while expected bankruptcy costs are worth $9.09091$; so
$V\le(83.63636)+(3.18182)-(9.09091)=77.72727<85.4545$ under any reading.
\answer{(e)} No: with a \$70 obligation the firm is worth \$76.53, less than \$85.45.

\intuition This is the static trade-off in miniature. Safe debt adds a small tax subsidy at no
cost. Debt large enough to default in the bad state buys a little more subsidy but triggers a
deadweight loss of \$20 with probability one half, which dominates.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Ignoring corporate tax in (a): $(105+33)/1.10=125.4545$ is the pre-tax value.
  \item Pricing the \$70 bond as safe, $70/1.10=63.64$: the bad state cannot pay it.
  \item Thinking the bankruptcy cost hurts only creditors: they price it into $D$, so the
        shareholders who issue the debt bear it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Risky debt in a binomial market and new debt of equal seniority --- Grinblatt \& Titman 14.2--14.3, pp.~526--527}

\asked
14.2: The share price of a firm worth \$210 or \$80 next period with \$50 of debt and 100 shares.
14.3: After a surprise issue of \$50 more face of equal-seniority debt used to buy back shares:
(a) the price of the new debt; (b) the share price right after the announcement; (c) show that a
20\% shareholder who undoes the leverage change ends up better off; (d) show that MM still holds.

\approach
With a zero interest rate and equal risk-neutral probabilities, every claim is worth the
plain average of its two payoffs. Equal seniority means that in default the cash is shared
pro rata by face value, so the new issue makes the old debt risky. \emph{MM Proposition I}
says total firm value does not depend on the split; any gain to shareholders must therefore be a
transfer from the old creditors. Undoing leverage is \emph{homemade leverage} run in reverse:
hold debt alongside the shares.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $C_G=210$, $C_B=80$: firm cash flow in the good/bad state; risk-neutral probability $\tfrac12$ each; $r_f=0$; no taxes.
  \item $F_0=50$: face of the existing debt; $F_1=50$: face of the new equal-seniority debt; 100 shares initially.
  \item $V$: firm (machine) value; $D_0$, $D_1$: market values of old and new debt; $P$: share price; $n_R$: shares repurchased.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{14.2: share price} With $r_f=0$, values are risk-neutral expectations:
\begin{align}
  V &= \tfrac12(210)+\tfrac12(80) = 105+40 = 145. \label{eq:11V}
\end{align}
The old debt is safe ($80\ge50$), so $D_0=50$; equity pays $(210-50,\ 80-50)=(160,\ 30)$ and is
worth $145-50=95$, so $P=95/100=0.95$.
\answer{(14.2)} $P=\$0.95$.

\step{2}{(a) Price of the new debt} Total face becomes $F_0+F_1=100>80$, so in the bad state the
\$80 is shared pro rata: each issue gets $\tfrac{50}{100}(80)=40$. The new debt pays $(50,\ 40)$:
\begin{align}
  D_1 &= \tfrac12(50)+\tfrac12(40) = 25+20 = 45. \label{eq:11D1}
\end{align}
The old debt now has the same payoff, so $D_0$ also falls from 50 to 45.
\answer{(a)} $D_1=\$45$.

\step{3}{(b) Share price at the announcement} Before the buyback the firm holds the machine plus
the \$45 cash raised and owes debt worth $2\times45=90$; the 100 shares are still outstanding:
\begin{align}
  E &= (145)+(45)-(90) = 100, \qquad P = \frac{100}{100} = 1.00. \label{eq:11P}
\end{align}
The firm then buys back $n_R=45/1.00=45$ shares; 55 remain. Equity pays
$(210-100,\ 0)=(110,\ 0)$, worth $\tfrac12(110)=55=145-90$, i.e.\ still \$1.00 per share.
\answer{(b)} The share price jumps from \$0.95 to \$1.00.

\assumption ``A shareholder with 20 percent of the shares'' holds 20 of the 100 shares before
the announcement; ``undoing the leverage change'' means rebuilding the pre-announcement payoff
exposure with personal trades.

\step{4}{(c) Undoing the leverage} Before, she held 20\% of an equity paying $(160,30)$, i.e.\
$(32,6)$, worth $\tfrac12(32)+\tfrac12(6)=16+3=19$. After the announcement her 20 shares are
worth 20. One way to undo the change: keep $0.2\times55=11$ shares (paying $11\times(2,0)=(22,0)$),
sell $20-11=9$ shares at \$1.00, and with the \$9 buy 20\% of the new debt, $0.2\times45=9$,
which pays $0.2\times(50,40)=(10,8)$. The position pays
\begin{align}
  (22+10,\ 0+8) = (32,\ 8), \qquad \text{worth } \tfrac12(32)+\tfrac12(8) = 20. \label{eq:11c}
\end{align}
This beats the old payoff $(32,6)$ state by state; the gain $20-19=1$ is 20\% of the \$5 lost by
the old creditors ($0.2\times5=1$).

To rebuild $(32,6)$ exactly, hold $n$ shares (each paying $(2,0)$) and a fraction $\beta$ of all
the debt (paying $\beta(100,80)$). The bad state gives $80\beta=6$, so $\beta=0.075$; the good
state gives $2n+(0.075)(100)=32$, so $2n=24.5$ and $n=12.25$. The cost is
$12.25(1.00)+0.075(90)=12.25+6.75=19.00$, and she keeps \$1 in cash from her \$20 of shares.
\answer{(c)} She can rebuild her old payoff $(32,6)$ for \$19 while her shares are worth \$20:
she is \$1 better off.

\step{5}{(d) MM still holds} Total firm value is $50+95=145$ before and $90+55=145$ after, as
\eqref{eq:11V} requires: changing the capital structure did not change the value of the
claims taken together, and investors can undo it (Step~4). The share price rose only because
equal-seniority debt diluted the old creditors, a transfer of \$5 from them to the
shareholders, not value creation.
\answer{(d)} Firm value is \$145 before and after; the \$1.00 price reflects a \$5 transfer from
old creditors, not new value.

\intuition MM is a statement about the size of the pie, not about how it is cut. Issuing new
debt that ranks equally with old debt cuts the old creditors' slice; without protective
covenants, shareholders pocket that slice.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pricing the new debt at its face \$50, ignoring that equal seniority makes all the debt risky.
  \item Dividing post-repurchase equity by 100 shares (or announcement equity by 55): the
        announcement price uses 100 shares and the cash still in the firm.
  \item Concluding that MM fails because the share price rose: the old creditors lost exactly what
        shareholders gained.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the book.
\refbib{Grinblatt \& Titman Ch.~14, §14.1 ``The Modigliani-Miller Theorem'' p.~501, §14.2 ``How an Individual Investor Can `Undo' a Firm's Capital Structure Choice'' p.~505, §14.3 ``How Risky Debt Affects the Modigliani-Miller Theorem'' pp.~506--508.}

\section{Leveraged recapitalisation with corporate taxes --- Berk 16.18, p.~493}

\asked
(a) The market value of Kurz's assets before the announcement.
(b) The market value of its assets, tax shield included, after the \$50 million is borrowed but
before any shares are bought back.
(c) The share price just before the buyback, and how many shares the \$50 million buys.
(d) The market-value balance sheet and share price after the buyback.

\approach
Build \emph{market-value balance sheets} at each date. Permanent interest-only debt creates an
interest tax shield worth $\tau_cD$ (\emph{MM I with taxes}), which is capitalised into the share
price as soon as the plan is known. The buyback then happens at that fair price, so it moves no
value between selling and remaining shareholders.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $N_0=20$ million shares at $P_0=7.50$; all equity before the announcement.
  \item $D=50$ million of permanent interest-only debt; $\tau_c=0.40$; $A_1$: assets after the issue; $P_1$: share price after the debt is issued; $n_R$: shares repurchased (millions).
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{(a) Existing assets} With no debt, assets equal equity:
\begin{align}
  N_0P_0 = (20)(7.50) = 150 \text{ million}. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(a)} \$150 million.

\step{2}{(b) Assets after the issue, before the repurchase} The firm holds the old assets, the
\$50 million cash, and the tax shield of permanent debt, $\tau_c D=(0.40)(50)=20$:
\begin{align}
  A_1 &= (150)+(50)+(20) = 220 \text{ million}. \label{eq:12A}
\end{align}
\answer{(b)} \$220 million (existing assets 150, cash 50, tax shield 20).

\step{3}{(c) Price and shares repurchased} Equity is assets minus debt, spread over the 20 million
shares still outstanding:
\begin{align}
  E_1 &= A_1-D = (220)-(50) = 170 \text{ million}, \nonumber\\
  P_1 &= \frac{E_1}{N_0} = \frac{170}{20} = 8.50, \label{eq:12P}\\
  n_R &= \frac{D}{P_1} = \frac{(50)}{(8.50)} = 5.882353 \text{ million shares}. \label{eq:12n}
\end{align}
\answer{(c)} $P_1=\$8.50$; Kurz repurchases 5.88 million shares.

\step{4}{(d) After the repurchase} The cash is gone and equity falls by the \$50 million paid out:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr@{\qquad}lr}
\toprule
Assets & (\$m) & Liabilities and equity & (\$m)\\
\midrule
Existing assets & 150 & Debt & 50\\
Tax shield & 20 & Equity & 120\\
\midrule
Total & 170 & Total & 170\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
With $20-5.882353=14.117647$ million shares left, the price is
\begin{align}
  P_2 = \frac{120}{14.117647} = 8.50, \nonumber
\end{align}
unchanged by the repurchase itself.
\answer{(d)} Assets 150 + 20 = 170 = debt 50 + equity 120 (\$m); share price \$8.50.

\intuition The whole tax benefit, \$20 million or \$1 per share, is capitalised into the price at
the announcement. Shareholders who sell in the buyback and those who stay share it equally, since
the repurchase is at the fair price \$8.50.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Repurchasing at \$7.50 ($50/7.50=6.6667$ million shares): once the plan is known the price
        already includes the tax shield.
  \item Leaving the \$50 million cash out of (b) (answering 170).
  \item Forgetting that the buyback itself does not change the price: only the announcement does.
\end{itemize}

\refbib{Berk Ch.~16, §16.3 ``Debt and Taxes'', pp.~473--479 (recapitalising to capture the tax shield).}

\section{Derive the MM adjusted cost of capital --- Ross 16.19, p.~578}

\asked
Prove that, with corporate taxes only, Ross's after-tax WACC (CMPC) equals $R_U[1-T_C(D/V)]$.

\approach
Combine three facts from Ross §16.4: the unlevered firm is a perpetuity $\mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)$
priced at $R_U$; \emph{MM I with taxes}, $V_L=V_U+T_CD$; and the definition of the CMPC. Multiplying
the CMPC by $V$ turns it into the total after-tax cash flow paid to investors net of the tax
saving, which is the unlevered cash flow. A second route goes through \emph{MM II with taxes}.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathit{EBIT}$: constant perpetual operating income, fully paid out; $T_C\in[0,1)$: corporate tax rate (no personal taxes).
  \item $D$: perpetual riskless debt with rate $R_D$; $E$: levered equity value; $V=E+D=V_L$: levered firm value; $V_U$: unlevered value.
  \item $R_U$: unlevered cost of capital; $R_E$: cost of levered equity; $\text{CMPC}=\tfrac{E}{V}R_E+\tfrac{D}{V}R_D(1-T_C)$ (Ross's WACC). Assume $E>0$, $V>0$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Claim.} $\text{CMPC}=R_U\bigl[1-T_C(D/V)\bigr]$.

\step{1}{Valuation identities (Ross §16.4)} The unlevered firm's perpetual cash flow is
$\mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)$, and $V_U=\mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)/R_U$; multiplying by $R_U$,
\begin{align}
  \mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C) = R_U V_U. \label{eq:13VU}
\end{align}
The shield $T_C R_D D$ is a perpetuity as risky as the debt, worth $T_C R_D D/R_D=T_C D$ (cf.\
\eqref{eq:3TS}), so MM I with taxes is
\begin{align}
  V = V_U + T_C D. \label{eq:13MM1}
\end{align}

\step{2}{Returns of the claims} Shareholders receive the after-tax income left after interest,
$(\mathit{EBIT}-R_D D)(1-T_C)$, forever; debtholders receive $R_D D$, a perpetuity worth $D$ at
rate $R_D$. Valuing the equity stream as a perpetuity, $E=(\mathit{EBIT}-R_D D)(1-T_C)/R_E$, so
\begin{align}
  E\,R_E &= (\mathit{EBIT}-R_D D)(1-T_C). \label{eq:13RE}
\end{align}

\step{3}{Multiply the CMPC by $V$} From the definition,
\begin{align}
  V\cdot\text{CMPC} &= E\,R_E + D\,R_D(1-T_C) \nonumber\\
     &= \bigl[(\mathit{EBIT}-R_D D)(1-T_C)\bigr] + R_D D(1-T_C) && \text{(substituting \eqref{eq:13RE})} \nonumber\\
     &= \mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)-R_DD(1-T_C)+R_DD(1-T_C) && \text{(expanding)} \nonumber\\
     &= \mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C) \nonumber\\
     &= \bigl[R_U V_U\bigr] && \text{(substituting \eqref{eq:13VU})} \nonumber\\
     &= R_U\bigl[V - T_C D\bigr] && \text{(substituting \eqref{eq:13MM1})}. \label{eq:13VW}
\end{align}

\step{4}{Divide by $V>0$}
\begin{align}
  \text{CMPC} = R_U\,\frac{V-T_CD}{V} = R_U\Bigl[1-T_C\frac{D}{V}\Bigr]. \qquad\blacksquare \label{eq:13res}
\end{align}
\answer{(proof)} $\text{CMPC}=R_U\bigl[1-T_C(D/V)\bigr]$, proved in \eqref{eq:13VW}--\eqref{eq:13res}.

\step{5}{Same result through MM II} First derive MM II with taxes. From \eqref{eq:13VU} and
\eqref{eq:13MM1}, $\mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)=R_UV_U=R_U(V-T_CD)=R_U(E+D-T_CD)$. Expand \eqref{eq:13RE}
and substitute:
\begin{align}
  E\,R_E &= \mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)-R_DD(1-T_C) \nonumber\\
     &= R_U(E+D-T_CD)-R_DD(1-T_C) && \text{(substituting)} \nonumber\\
     &= R_UE+R_UD(1-T_C)-R_DD(1-T_C) \nonumber\\
     &= R_UE+(R_U-R_D)D(1-T_C). \nonumber
\end{align}
Dividing by $E$ gives Ross's [16.4], $R_E=R_U+(R_U-R_D)\tfrac{D}{E}(1-T_C)$. Substitute it into
the CMPC:
\begin{align}
  \text{CMPC} &= \tfrac{E}{V}\bigl[R_U+(R_U-R_D)\tfrac{D}{E}(1-T_C)\bigr]+\tfrac{D}{V}R_D(1-T_C) \nonumber\\
     &= \tfrac{E}{V}R_U+\tfrac{D}{V}(R_U-R_D)(1-T_C)+\tfrac{D}{V}R_D(1-T_C) \nonumber\\
     &= \tfrac{E}{V}R_U+\tfrac{D}{V}R_U(1-T_C) \nonumber\\
     &= R_U\Bigl[\tfrac{E+D}{V}-T_C\tfrac{D}{V}\Bigr] = R_U\Bigl[1-T_C\tfrac{D}{V}\Bigr], \nonumber
\end{align}
since $E+D=V$.

\step{6}{Numerical check with Exercise 3} There $T_CD/V=21{,}000/419{,}666.67=0.05003971$, so
$\text{CMPC}=(0.15)(1-0.05003971)=0.14249404$, and $(0.14249404)(419{,}666.67)=59{,}800.00=\mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)$,
as \eqref{eq:13VW} requires. Directly: equity income is $(92{,}000-5{,}400)(0.65)=56{,}290$, so
$R_E=56{,}290/359{,}666.67=0.156506$ (MM II gives the same:
$0.15+(0.06)(0.166821)(0.65)=0.15+0.006506$), and with $E/V=0.857029$, $D/V=0.142971$,
\begin{align}
  \text{CMPC} &= (0.857029)(0.156506)+(0.142971)(0.09)(0.65) = 0.134130+0.008364 = 0.142494. \nonumber
\end{align}

\intuition The levered firm's investors, taken together, receive the unlevered cash flow
$\mathit{EBIT}(1-T_C)$ \emph{plus} the tax saving; the CMPC nets the saving out of the debt cost,
so it must discount the unlevered cash flow to the levered value. Since $V$ exceeds $V_U$ by
$T_CD$, the rate falls below $R_U$ by exactly $R_U T_C D/V$. The formula relies on fixed
perpetual debt; under a constant leverage ratio the shield is riskier and the formula changes.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Using MM II without taxes, $R_E=R_U+(R_U-R_D)D/E$: then the CMPC does not reduce to the claim.
  \item Confusing $V$ with $V_U$ in $D/V$: the weights are levered market values.
  \item Applying the formula to a firm that keeps $D/V$ constant: it is derived for fixed perpetual debt.
\end{itemize}

\bookans None in the local copy (selected answers are in the web-only Apêndice C).
\refbib{Ross Cap.~16, §16.4, pp.~551--556 (Proposition I with taxes; taxes, WACC and Proposition II).}

\section{Miller's gain from leverage with personal taxes --- Amaro de Matos 2.2.7 \#1, p.~58}

\asked
Prove that, with corporate tax $\tau_c$ and personal taxes $\tau_s$ on equity income and $\tau_b$
on interest, the value that leverage creates for stockholders is
$G_L=\bigl[1-(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)/(1-\tau_b)\bigr]B$.

\approach
Use the \emph{homemade-leverage} replication of MM, now counting every tax on each income
stream (Miller 1977). An investor can copy the after-tax income of the levered firm's equity by
holding the unlevered firm's equity and borrowing personally; since interest she pays is
deductible at $\tau_b$, she must borrow less than the firm's share of debt. No arbitrage then
pins down $S_l$, and $G_L=V_l-V_u$ follows.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $X$: random operating income per period of two otherwise identical firms, $u$ (unlevered) and $l$ (levered), forever.
  \item $B$: market value of $l$'s perpetual riskless debt, which pays $rB$ per period; $r>0$ is the bond rate.
  \item $\tau_c,\tau_s,\tau_b\in[0,1)$: corporate tax rate (interest deductible), personal tax rate on equity income, personal tax rate on interest income.
  \item $S_u=V_u$, $S_l$: equity values; $V_l=S_l+B$; $\alpha\in(0,1]$: fraction held; $L$: personal loan; $G_L=V_l-V_u$: gain from leverage.
\end{itemize}

\assumption As in Amaro's §2.2 and Appendix answer, individuals borrow and lend at the bond rate
$r$, and personal interest is taxed (lending) or deductible (borrowing) at $\tau_b$; there are no
transaction costs and no arbitrage.

\step{1}{Payoff of the levered equity} The firm pays corporate tax on $X-rB$, and the holder pays $\tau_s$ on what she receives:
\begin{align}
  Y_l = \alpha(X-rB)(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s). \label{eq:14Yl}
\end{align}

\step{2}{Payoff of the unlevered equity} The same taxes apply to the whole $X$:
\begin{align}
  Y_u = \alpha X(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s). \label{eq:14Yu}
\end{align}

\step{3}{Size of the personal loan} A loan $L$ at $r$ costs $rL$ in interest, of which $\tau_b rL$
comes back as a tax deduction, so $rL(1-\tau_b)$ per period after tax. Choose $L$ so this equals
the debt-service part of \eqref{eq:14Yl}, $\alpha rB(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)$:
\begin{align}
  rL(1-\tau_b) &= \alpha rB(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s) \nonumber\\
  L &= \alpha B\,\frac{(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)}{1-\tau_b} && \text{(dividing by } r(1-\tau_b)>0). \label{eq:14L}
\end{align}

\step{4}{Replication} Buying $\alpha$ of $u$ and borrowing $L$ pays, by \eqref{eq:14Yu} and \eqref{eq:14L},
\begin{align}
  Y_u - rL(1-\tau_b) &= \alpha X(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)-\bigl[\alpha rB(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)\bigr] \nonumber\\
     &= \alpha(X-rB)(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s) = Y_l, \nonumber
\end{align}
in every state and every period. Its cost today is $\alpha S_u-L$.

\step{5}{Law of one price} If $\alpha S_l>\alpha S_u-L$, a holder of $l$'s shares sells them, builds the
replica and keeps the difference with unchanged future income. If $\alpha S_l<\alpha S_u-L$, a holder
of $u$'s shares sells them, buys $\alpha$ of $l$'s shares and lends $L$ (after-tax interest
$rL(1-\tau_b)$, restoring $Y_u$ by Step~4 read backwards), again pocketing the difference.
No arbitrage therefore requires
\begin{align}
  \alpha S_l &= \alpha S_u - L \nonumber\\
  \alpha S_l &= \alpha S_u - \alpha B\,\frac{(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)}{1-\tau_b} && \text{(substituting \eqref{eq:14L})} \nonumber\\
  S_l &= S_u - B\,\frac{(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)}{1-\tau_b} && \text{(dividing by } \alpha). \label{eq:14Sl}
\end{align}

\step{6}{Firm values and the gain} Add $B$ to both sides of \eqref{eq:14Sl} and use $V_l=S_l+B$, $V_u=S_u$:
\begin{align}
  V_l &= V_u + B - B\,\frac{(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)}{1-\tau_b}, \nonumber\\
  G_L = V_l - V_u &= \Bigl[1-\frac{(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)}{1-\tau_b}\Bigr]B. \qquad\blacksquare \label{eq:14GL}
\end{align}
The gain goes to the stockholders who decide to lever: they collect $B$ at issue and keep shares
worth $S_l$, a total of $V_l$ instead of $V_u$.
\answer{(proof)} $G_L=\bigl[1-(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)/(1-\tau_b)\bigr]B$, proved in \eqref{eq:14GL}.

\step{7}{Special cases} (i) $\tau_s=\tau_b$ (including no personal taxes): $G_L=\tau_cB$, the MM
(1963) result, Amaro (2.7). (ii) Miller's equilibrium (2.11), $(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)=1-\tau_b$:
$G_L=0$, so no single firm gains from leverage. (iii) $(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)>1-\tau_b$: $G_L<0$,
and the firm should not borrow. Illustration: with $\tau_c=0.34$, $\tau_s=0.15$, $\tau_b=0.30$,
$(0.66)(0.85)/0.70=0.561/0.70=0.801429$, so $G_L=0.198571\,B$, well below $\tau_cB=0.34\,B$.

\intuition Debt moves a dollar of income from the corporate-plus-equity tax channel, where it
keeps $(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)$, to the interest channel, where it keeps $(1-\tau_b)$. Leverage
creates value only to the extent that the second channel is less taxed than the first.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Borrowing $\alpha B$ (the firm's share of debt) instead of $L$ in \eqref{eq:14L}: the
        personal deduction at $\tau_b$ changes the size of the loan that copies the firm's debt service.
  \item The sign of the loan: borrowing \emph{reduces} the cost of the replica, so $\alpha S_l=\alpha S_u-L$.
  \item Concluding that $G_L=\tau_cB$ in general: that holds only when $\tau_s=\tau_b$.
\end{itemize}

\bookans The final expression matches the book's answer (p.~255). The intermediate line printed
there, ``$\alpha S_l=\alpha S_u+\alpha B\frac{(1-\tau_c)(1-\tau_s)}{(1-\tau_b)}$'', has a sign typo:
it must be a minus, as in \eqref{eq:14Sl}, since the investor \emph{borrows} that amount and the
book's own next line, $V_l=V_u+B[1-\cdots]$, follows only with the minus.
\refbib{Amaro de Matos Ch.~2, §2.2 ``Personal and Corporate Taxation'', pp.~48--52 (Miller equilibrium, $G_L$ on p.~52); Appendix C.2.2, p.~255.}

\section{MM I with perpetual debt, and when borrowing and lending rates differ --- Amaro de Matos 2.1.5 \#3--\#4, p.~48}

\asked
\#3: Prove MM I in the original 1958 setting (perpetual debt paying $rD$ each period, income
measured as a perpetual average $X$), both without taxes ($V_l=V_u$) and with deductible
interest ($V_l=V_u+\tau B$).
\#4: What becomes of MM I if individuals borrow at $\bar r$ and lend at $\underline r$, with
$\bar r>r>\underline r$, while firms borrow at $r$.

\approach
Both parts use the \emph{homemade-leverage} arbitrage. For each firm's equity, build a
portfolio from the other firm's securities plus personal borrowing or lending that pays the same
income in every state; no arbitrage then forces the two to cost the same, which gives two
inequalities whose combination is MM I. In \#4 personal borrowing is dearer and lending cheaper
than corporate debt, so the replicas cost more and the two inequalities no longer meet: they
leave a band.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $X$: random operating income per period (the time average of the stream), identical for firms $u$ (unlevered) and $l$ (levered); any distribution.
  \item $B$: market value of $l$'s perpetual riskless debt, paying $rB$ per period; $r$: corporate interest rate.
  \item $\tau\in[0,1)$: corporate tax rate, interest deductible ($\tau=0$ is the no-tax case); no personal taxes.
  \item $V_u=S_u$, $V_l=S_l+B$; $\alpha\in(0,1]$: fraction of a firm held by an investor.
  \item \#4 only: $\bar r$ and $\underline r$: individual borrowing and lending rates, $\bar r>r>\underline r>0$.
\end{itemize}

\assumption In \#3 individuals borrow and lend at $r$, as firms do; there are no transaction
costs. In \#4, as in the book's answer, $\tau=0$ and individuals can lend only at $\underline r$
(they cannot buy $l$'s bonds at yield $r$).

\subsection*{\#3 --- MM I with and without taxes}

\step{1}{Payoffs} Shareholders of $u$ pay tax on all of $X$; those of $l$ pay tax on $X$ net of
interest. Each period they receive
\begin{align}
  Y_u = X(1-\tau), \qquad Y_l = (X-rB)(1-\tau). \label{eq:15Y}
\end{align}

\step{2}{Replicating $l$'s equity} Buy $\alpha$ of $u$ and borrow $\alpha(1-\tau)B$ at $r$. By \eqref{eq:15Y} the income is
\begin{align}
  \alpha X(1-\tau) - r\,\alpha(1-\tau)B = \alpha(X-rB)(1-\tau) = \alpha Y_l, \nonumber
\end{align}
at a cost of $\alpha S_u-\alpha(1-\tau)B$ today.

\step{3}{Upper bound} If $S_l>S_u-(1-\tau)B$, a holder of $\alpha$ of $l$'s shares sells them for
$\alpha S_l$, builds the replica of Step~2 and keeps, with identical future income,
\begin{align}
  \alpha\bigl[S_l-S_u+(1-\tau)B\bigr] &= \alpha\bigl[(V_l-B)-V_u+(1-\tau)B\bigr] && \text{(using } S_l=V_l-B,\ S_u=V_u) \nonumber\\
      &= \alpha\bigl[V_l-V_u-B+B-\tau B\bigr] \nonumber\\
      &= \alpha\bigl[V_l-V_u-\tau B\bigr] > 0, \nonumber
\end{align}
an arbitrage. Hence $V_l\le V_u+\tau B$.

\step{4}{Replicating $u$'s equity} Buy $\alpha$ of $l$'s shares and $\alpha(1-\tau)$ of its debt (or lend $\alpha(1-\tau)B$ at $r$):
\begin{align}
  \alpha(X-rB)(1-\tau)+\alpha(1-\tau)rB = \alpha X(1-\tau) = \alpha Y_u, \nonumber
\end{align}
at a cost of $\alpha\bigl[S_l+(1-\tau)B\bigr]$.

\step{5}{Lower bound} If $S_u>S_l+(1-\tau)B$, then substituting $S_u=V_u$, $S_l=V_l-B$ gives
$V_u>V_l-B+B-\tau B$, i.e.\ $V_l<V_u+\tau B$. A holder of $u$'s shares then sells them,
buys the replica of Step~4 and keeps $\alpha[V_u+\tau B-V_l]>0$. Hence $V_l\ge V_u+\tau B$.

\step{6}{MM I} Steps 3 and 5 give
\begin{align}
  V_l = V_u + \tau B \quad\text{(with taxes)}, \qquad V_l = V_u \quad (\tau=0). \qquad\blacksquare \label{eq:15MM1}
\end{align}
\answer{(\#3)} $V_l=V_u+\tau B$ with deductible interest; $V_l=V_u$ without taxes.

\bookans Matches the book's answer (pp.~252--253), $V_u+\tau B=V_l$. The book's switching
portfolio invests all the proceeds in the other firm instead of replicating exactly, which leads
to the same two inequalities.

\subsection*{\#4 --- Different borrowing and lending rates ($\tau=0$)}

\step{7}{Replicating $l$'s equity with costly borrowing} The replica must still hold $\alpha$ of $u$
(the only other claim on $X$), so its debt service has to equal $\alpha rB$ at the rate $\bar r$:
\begin{align}
  \bar r\,\bar L = \alpha rB \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar L = \alpha B\,\frac{r}{\bar r}, \label{eq:15Lbar}
\end{align}
which gives income $\alpha X-\bar r\bar L=\alpha X-\alpha rB=\alpha Y_l$ at a cost of
$\alpha S_u-\bar L=\alpha\bigl(S_u-B\,r/\bar r\bigr)$.
(Borrowing the full $\alpha B$ would cost $\alpha\bar rB>\alpha rB$ per period: not a replica.)

\step{8}{Upper bound} No arbitrage against an overvalued $l$ requires
$\alpha S_l\le\alpha\bigl(S_u-B\,r/\bar r\bigr)$, i.e.\ $S_l\le S_u-B\,r/\bar r$. Add $B$ to both
sides and use $V_l=S_l+B$, $V_u=S_u$:
\begin{align}
  V_l \le V_u + B - B\,\frac{r}{\bar r} = V_u + B\Bigl(1-\frac{r}{\bar r}\Bigr) = V_u + B\,\frac{\bar r-r}{\bar r}. \label{eq:15up}
\end{align}

\step{9}{Replicating $u$'s equity with poor lending} Buy $\alpha$ of $l$'s shares and lend $\underline L$ at $\underline r$ with
\begin{align}
  \underline r\,\underline L = \alpha rB \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \underline L = \alpha B\,\frac{r}{\underline r}>\alpha B, \label{eq:15Llow}
\end{align}
giving income $\alpha(X-rB)+\alpha rB=\alpha Y_u$ at a cost of $\alpha\bigl(S_l+B\,r/\underline r\bigr)$.

\step{10}{Lower bound} No arbitrage against an overvalued $u$ requires $S_u\le S_l+B\,r/\underline r$.
Substitute $S_u=V_u$ and $S_l=V_l-B$:
\begin{align}
  V_u &\le V_l-B+B\,\frac{r}{\underline r} \nonumber\\
  V_l &\ge V_u+B-B\,\frac{r}{\underline r} = V_u - B\Bigl(\frac{r}{\underline r}-1\Bigr) = V_u - B\,\frac{r-\underline r}{\underline r}. \label{eq:15low}
\end{align}

\step{11}{What happens to MM1} Combining \eqref{eq:15up} and \eqref{eq:15low},
\begin{align}
  V_u - B\,\frac{r-\underline r}{\underline r} \;\le\; V_l \;\le\; V_u + B\,\frac{\bar r-r}{\bar r}. \qquad\blacksquare \label{eq:15band}
\end{align}
MM1 no longer pins down $V_l$: arbitrage only confines it to a band around $V_u$ whose width grows
with the debt $B$ and with the spreads. With $\bar r=r+\epsilon$, $\underline r=r-\epsilon$ the band is
$V_u-\epsilon B/(r-\epsilon)\le V_l\le V_u+\epsilon B/(r+\epsilon)$, and it collapses to $V_l=V_u$
as $\epsilon\to0$. Leverage can now raise firm value (up to the upper bound): the firm borrows
more cheaply than individuals, so corporate leverage is a service investors cannot fully copy.
If individuals could buy $l$'s bonds at yield $r$, Step~4 would apply and the lower bound would
tighten to $V_l\ge V_u$.
\answer{(\#4)} MM1 fails as an equality: $V_l$ is only confined to the band \eqref{eq:15band}
around $V_u$, which shrinks to $V_l=V_u$ as the spreads vanish.

\intuition MM1 rests on investors being able to copy corporate leverage on the same terms. When
personal borrowing is dearer and lending cheaper than corporate debt, homemade leverage is an
imperfect substitute: arbitrage still limits mispricing, but only to within the cost of the
personal-borrowing wedge.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item In \#3 with taxes, borrowing $\alpha B$ instead of $\alpha(1-\tau)B$: the firm's interest is
        tax-deductible, so its equity bears only $(1-\tau)rB$ of debt service.
  \item In \#4, borrowing the full $\alpha B$ at $\bar r$ and calling it a replica: the debt
        service then differs, so the incomes do not match state by state.
  \item Concluding that MM1 ``fails completely'': arbitrage still bounds $V_l$, only less tightly.
\end{itemize}

\bookans Differs from the book's printed band (p.~254), $V_u(1-\epsilon B_l/X)\le V_l\le V_u(1+\epsilon B_l/X)$.
The qualitative conclusion is the same (a band around $V_u$, proportional to $\epsilon B$, vanishing at
$\epsilon=0$), but the bounds differ. The book's portfolios keep the investor's capital fixed (all
proceeds go into the other firm, and the full principal $B_l$ is borrowed or lent), so the two
incomes differ by a multiple of the random $X$ minus a fixed amount. Its inequality therefore holds
only for a given value of $X$ (or in expectation), not state by state. The portfolios above match
incomes exactly, so \eqref{eq:15band} is a true no-arbitrage band for any distribution of $X$. The
two coincide only if $X/V_u$ happens to equal $\bar r$ (upper bound) or $\underline r$ (lower bound).
\refbib{Amaro de Matos Ch.~2, §2.1.1--2.1.3, pp.~40--46 (MM1, MM2, MM with taxes); Appendix C.2.1, pp.~252--254.}

\section{Bank capital without taxes: does more equity lower ROE and raise funding costs? --- Original exercise}

\noindent\textit{Original exercise (list, Exercise 16), built on Topic 2B slides 46--47 and Topic 2A
slides 89 and 97--102. Amounts in \$ billion.}

\asked
(a) The balance sheet at the two equity ratios and a check that deposits are really safe.
(b) What shareholders earn per dollar invested (ROE) in each state, its mean and its risk, and the
reward per unit of risk.
(c) The return shareholders \emph{require} at each ratio (MM II), whether it matches what they
expect to earn, and whether the overall cost of funding (WACC) or the bank's value moves.
(d) How a shareholder can rebuild the high-leverage ROE herself by borrowing.
(e)--(f) Whether Statements 1 and 2 of slide 47 survive these numbers.

\approach
The bank is a firm in a perfect market, so MM applies unchanged: the loans' cash flows and their
required return $r_A$ do not depend on how they are funded (\emph{MM I}). Leverage only
redistributes the same risk: with less equity, each dollar of equity carries more of the asset risk
and therefore must earn more (\emph{MM II}). ROE compares returns on claims of different risk, so a
lower ROE at higher capital is not a loss. \emph{Homemade leverage} shows the old ROE remains
available to any investor who wants it.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $A=100$: market value of the loans at $t=0$; $\tilde r_A\in\{9\%,3\%\}$: return on assets (good, bad state), probability $\tfrac12$ each; $r_A=6\%$: required return on assets; $\sigma_A$: standard deviation of $\tilde r_A$.
  \item $e=E/A$: equity ratio; $E$, $D=A-E$: equity and deposits; $r_D=r_f=4\%$: deposit (risk-free) rate.
  \item $\Pi_s$: payoff to shareholders at $t=1$ in state $s\in\{g,b\}$; $ROE_s=\Pi_s/E-1$; $r_E$: required return on equity; $r_{WACC}$: weighted average cost of capital.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{(a) Balance sheets and the repayment owed} The equity ratio fixes $E=eA$, deposits are the
rest, and depositors are owed principal plus interest:
\begin{align}
  e=4\%:&\quad E=(0.04)(100)=4,\quad D=100-4=96,\quad D(1+r_f)=(96)(1.04)=99.84, \label{eq:16owed}\\
  e=20\%:&\quad E=(0.20)(100)=20,\quad D=100-20=80,\quad D(1+r_f)=(80)(1.04)=83.2. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{2}{(a) Deposits are safe} The worst case for depositors is the bad state, where the loans pay
\begin{align}
  A(1+0.03)=(100)(1.03)=103. \label{eq:16Ab}
\end{align}
Since $103>99.84$ and $103>83.2$, depositors are paid in full in both states at both ratios, so the
deposits are risk-free and $r_D=r_f$ is the right rate. In the good state the loans pay
$(100)(1.09)=109$.
\answer{(a)} $e=4\%$: $E=4$, $D=96$, owed $99.84$; $e=20\%$: $E=20$, $D=80$, owed $83.2$; both below
the bad-state asset payoff of $103$, so deposits are risk-free.

\step{3}{(b) Shareholders' payoffs} Shareholders receive what is left after depositors:
\begin{align}
  e=4\%:&\quad \Pi_g=109-99.84=9.16,\qquad \Pi_b=103-99.84=3.16, \nonumber\\
  e=20\%:&\quad \Pi_g=109-83.2=25.8,\qquad\ \Pi_b=103-83.2=19.8. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{4}{(b) ROE in each state} Divide each payoff by the equity invested and subtract 1:
\begin{align}
  e=4\%:&\quad ROE_g=\frac{9.16}{4}-1=2.29-1=129\%,\qquad ROE_b=\frac{3.16}{4}-1=0.79-1=-21\%, \nonumber\\
  e=20\%:&\quad ROE_g=\frac{25.8}{20}-1=1.29-1=29\%,\qquad\ \ ROE_b=\frac{19.8}{20}-1=0.99-1=-1\%. \nonumber
\end{align}
The same numbers follow from $ROE_s=\tilde r_{A,s}+\frac{D}{E}(\tilde r_{A,s}-r_f)$: at $e=4\%$,
$D/E=96/4=24$ and $ROE_g=9\%+24(9\%-4\%)=9\%+120\%=129\%$.

\step{5}{(b) Mean, risk and reward per unit of risk} With two equally likely states the mean is the
average and the standard deviation is half the gap between the two outcomes:
\begin{align}
  e=4\%:&\quad \E[ROE]=\tfrac12(129\%)+\tfrac12(-21\%)=54\%, \label{eq:16mom}\\
        &\quad \sigma(ROE)=\tfrac12\,|129\%-(-21\%)|=\tfrac12(150\%)=75\%, \nonumber\\
  e=20\%:&\quad \E[ROE]=\tfrac12(29\%)+\tfrac12(-1\%)=14\%, \nonumber\\
        &\quad \sigma(ROE)=\tfrac12\,|29\%-(-1\%)|=\tfrac12(30\%)=15\%. \nonumber
\end{align}
For comparison, $\sigma_A=\tfrac12|9\%-3\%|=3\%$, and $\sigma(ROE)=(A/E)\,\sigma_A$: $(25)(3\%)=75\%$ and
$(5)(3\%)=15\%$. The reward per unit of risk is
\begin{align}
  e=4\%:\quad \frac{54\%-4\%}{75\%}=\frac{50}{75}=0.6667,\qquad
  e=20\%:\quad \frac{14\%-4\%}{15\%}=\frac{10}{15}=0.6667. \label{eq:16sharpe}
\end{align}
\answer{(b)} $e=4\%$: ROE $129\%$ / $-21\%$, $\E[ROE]=54\%$, $\sigma=75\%$; $e=20\%$: ROE $29\%$ /
$-1\%$, $\E[ROE]=14\%$, $\sigma=15\%$. The ratio is $0.6667$ at both: expected ROE falls exactly in
proportion to risk.

\step{6}{(c) Required return on equity (MM II)} With safe debt, $r_E=r_A+\frac{D}{E}(r_A-r_D)$:
\begin{align}
  e=4\%:&\quad r_E=6\%+\Bigl(\frac{96}{4}\Bigr)(6\%-4\%)=6\%+(24)(2\%)=6\%+48\%=54\%, \label{eq:16rE}\\
  e=20\%:&\quad r_E=6\%+\Bigl(\frac{80}{20}\Bigr)(6\%-4\%)=6\%+(4)(2\%)=6\%+8\%=14\%. \nonumber
\end{align}
These equal the expected ROEs in \eqref{eq:16mom}: at both ratios shareholders expect to earn
exactly what their risk requires, so their claim has zero NPV either way. Indeed the equity is worth
$\E[\Pi]/(1+r_E)$: $\tfrac12(9.16+3.16)/1.54=6.16/1.54=4$ and $\tfrac12(25.8+19.8)/1.14=22.8/1.14=20$, the
amounts invested.

\step{7}{(c) WACC and value} Weight each required return by its share of funding:
\begin{align}
  e=4\%:&\quad r_{WACC}=(0.04)(54\%)+(0.96)(4\%)=2.16\%+3.84\%=6\%, \label{eq:16wacc}\\
  e=20\%:&\quad r_{WACC}=(0.20)(14\%)+(0.80)(4\%)=2.80\%+3.20\%=6\%. \nonumber
\end{align}
Both equal $r_A$. The bank's value is $E+D=4+96=20+80=100=A$ at both ratios.
\answer{(c)} $r_E=54\%$ at $4\%$ equity and $14\%$ at $20\%$ equity, equal to the expected ROEs;
$r_{WACC}=6\%$ at both; the bank's value stays $100$.

\step{8}{(d) Homemade leverage} One dollar of the $4\%$ bank's equity carries $A/E=1/0.04=25$ dollars of
loans; one dollar of the $20\%$ bank's equity carries $1/0.20=5$. To rebuild 25 dollars of loan exposure
per dollar of her own money, she buys $25/5=5$ dollars of the $20\%$ bank's equity, financed with her
\$1 plus $5-1=4$ dollars borrowed at $r_f$. Her return in each state:
\begin{align}
  \text{good:}\quad &\frac{(5)(1.29)-(4)(1.04)}{1}-1=6.45-4.16-1=1.29=129\%, \nonumber\\
  \text{bad:}\quad &\frac{(5)(0.99)-(4)(1.04)}{1}-1=4.95-4.16-1=-0.21=-21\%. \label{eq:16home}
\end{align}
\answer{(d)} Buy \$5 of the $20\%$ bank's equity per \$1 of her own money, borrowing \$4 at $4\%$; she
gets $129\%$ and $-21\%$, exactly the $4\%$ bank's ROE.

\step{9}{(e) Statement 1} Expected ROE does fall, from $54\%$ to $14\%$, but risk falls in the same
proportion \eqref{eq:16sharpe}, the required return falls by the same amount \eqref{eq:16rE}, the value of
the shareholders' claim equals what they paid at both ratios, and the high-ROE payoff is still
available through \eqref{eq:16home}.
\answer{(e)} False. A lower ROE is the price of lower risk, not a loss to shareholders.

\step{10}{(f) Statement 2} The executive's computation keeps $r_E$ at its $4\%$-equity level:
\begin{align}
  (0.20)(54\%)+(0.80)(4\%)=10.8\%+3.2\%=14\%. \label{eq:16wrong}
\end{align}
But $r_E$ is not a constant: by MM II it falls to $14\%$ when equity rises to $20\%$, and the correct
WACC \eqref{eq:16wacc} stays at $6\%$. Equity has a higher required return than debt at every ratio,
yet replacing debt with equity does not raise the funding cost, because it makes all equity less
risky.
\answer{(f)} The executive's number is $14\%$; the error is holding $r_E$ fixed at $54\%$. Statement 2
is false: the funding cost stays at $6\%$.

\intuition The loans' risk is fixed by the loans, not by the liability side. Leverage concentrates
that risk on a thin layer of equity, which then demands a high return; more equity spreads the same
risk over more dollars, so each dollar demands less. ROE is a return on a risky claim, and comparing
ROEs across leverage levels compares claims of different risk.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Judging shareholder welfare by expected ROE instead of by the value of their claim (or ROE
        per unit of risk).
  \item Using a fixed $r_E$ when leverage changes, as in \eqref{eq:16wrong}.
  \item Forgetting to check that deposits are safe; MM II in the linear form needs $r_D=r_f$.
\end{itemize}

\refbib{Prof.~Ricca, Topic 2B slides 46--47 (bank capital debate, Statements 1--2); Topic 2A slide 89
(homemade leverage) and slides 97--102 (MM II, $r_{WACC}$ independent of $D$ and $E$, conservation of value).}

\section{Bank capital with corporate taxes: the lost interest tax shield --- Original exercise}

\noindent\textit{Original exercise (list, Exercise 17), built on Topic 2B slides 7--18 and 47.
Amounts in \$ billion, flows per year.}

\asked
(a) The bank's market-value balance sheet at $5\%$ and $20\%$ equity when interest is tax-deductible.
(b) What the move to $20\%$ requires (debt retired) and what it costs the bank's current owners.
(c) How the after-tax WACC and the required return on equity change, with two consistency checks.
(d) What happens to the government's tax claim and to the total value of all claims.
(e) Whether the lost tax shield (Statement 3) is a private cost, a social cost, or both.

\approach
With constant perpetual safe debt, \emph{MM I with taxes} gives $V_L=V_U+\tau D$ (slides 7--8): the
interest tax shield is the only thing leverage adds. Because the requirement is stated as a share of
$V_L$, which itself depends on $D$, part (a) is a fixed point solved in one line. Part (d) counts the
government as a third claimant: the pie $X$ is split among shareholders, debtholders and the
government, and leverage only moves value between them.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $X=7.5$: expected pre-tax cash flow per year (perpetual); $\tau=20\%$: tax rate; $r_U=6\%$: unlevered cost of capital; $V_U=(1-\tau)X/r_U=100$.
  \item $D$: perpetual constant safe debt, $r_D=4\%$; $E$: equity; $V_L=D+E$; $e=E/V_L$; $V_{ITS}=\tau D$.
  \item $r_E$: required return on equity; $r_{WACC}$: after-tax WACC; $G$: present value of the government's tax claim.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{(a) Solve for $V_L$} Substitute $D=(1-e)V_L$ into $V_L=V_U+\tau D$ and collect $V_L$:
\begin{align}
  V_L &= V_U+\tau(1-e)V_L \nonumber\\
  V_L\bigl[1-\tau(1-e)\bigr] &= V_U \nonumber\\
  V_L &= \frac{V_U}{1-\tau(1-e)}. \label{eq:17VL}
\end{align}

\step{2}{(a) Balance sheet at $e=5\%$}
\begin{align}
  V_L &= \frac{100}{1-(0.20)(0.95)}=\frac{100}{1-0.19}=\frac{100}{0.81}=123.4568, \nonumber\\
  D &= (0.95)(123.4568)=117.2840,\qquad E=(0.05)(123.4568)=6.1728, \nonumber\\
  V_{ITS} &= (0.20)(117.2840)=23.4568=V_L-V_U. \nonumber
\end{align}

\step{3}{(a) Balance sheet at $e=20\%$}
\begin{align}
  V_L &= \frac{100}{1-(0.20)(0.80)}=\frac{100}{1-0.16}=\frac{100}{0.84}=119.0476, \nonumber\\
  D &= (0.80)(119.0476)=95.2381,\qquad E=(0.20)(119.0476)=23.8095, \nonumber\\
  V_{ITS} &= (0.20)(95.2381)=19.0476=V_L-V_U. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(a)} $e=5\%$: $V_L=123.457$, $D=117.284$, $E=6.173$, $V_{ITS}=23.457$; $e=20\%$:
$V_L=119.048$, $D=95.238$, $E=23.810$, $V_{ITS}=19.048$.

\step{4}{(b) Debt retired and the annual tax saving} The debt retired equals the new equity raised:
\begin{align}
  \Delta D=117.2840-95.2381=22.0459. \nonumber
\end{align}
The annual interest tax saving $\tau r_D D$ is
\begin{align}
  e=5\%:\ (0.20)(0.04)(117.2840)=0.9383,\qquad e=20\%:\ (0.20)(0.04)(95.2381)=0.7619, \nonumber
\end{align}
a fall of $0.9383-0.7619=0.1764$ per year.

\step{5}{(b) Loss to existing shareholders} New shareholders pay a fair price, so they pay exactly
what their shares are worth and neither gain nor lose. The fall in $V_L$ therefore lands entirely on the existing shareholders:
\begin{align}
  \Delta V_L &= 123.4568-119.0476=4.4092=\tau\,\Delta D=(0.20)(22.0459), \label{eq:17loss}
\end{align}
which is also the perpetual annual loss discounted at $r_D$: $0.1764/0.04=4.41$. The existing
shareholders keep $23.8095-22.0459=1.7637=6.1728-4.4092$, so the loss as a fraction of their equity is
\begin{align}
  \frac{4.4092}{6.1728}=0.7143=71.43\%. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(b)} Retire $22.046$ of debt; the annual tax saving falls from $0.938$ to $0.762$; existing
shareholders lose $4.409$, i.e.\ $71.43\%$ of their pre-change equity.

\step{6}{(c) After-tax WACC} Constant-debt formula $r_{WACC}=\bigl(1-\tau\frac{D}{V_L}\bigr)r_U$, with $D/V_L=1-e$:
\begin{align}
  e=5\%:&\quad r_{WACC}=\bigl(1-(0.20)(0.95)\bigr)(6\%)=(0.81)(6\%)=4.86\%, \nonumber\\
        &\quad \frac{(1-\tau)X}{r_{WACC}}=\frac{6}{0.0486}=123.4568, \nonumber\\
  e=20\%:&\quad r_{WACC}=\bigl(1-(0.20)(0.80)\bigr)(6\%)=(0.84)(6\%)=5.04\%, \nonumber\\
        &\quad \frac{(1-\tau)X}{r_{WACC}}=\frac{6}{0.0504}=119.0476. \nonumber
\end{align}
Both reproduce $V_L$ from Steps 2--3. The after-tax WACC rises by $5.04\%-4.86\%=0.18$ percentage points.

\step{7}{(c) Required return on equity} The balance-sheet identity (slide 10) multiplied by $V_L$, with
$r_{ITS}=r_D$ and $V_{ITS}=\tau D$, is $E\,r_E+D\,r_D=V_U r_U+\tau D\,r_D$. Substitute $V_U=V_L-\tau D=E+(1-\tau)D$ and solve for $r_E$:
\begin{align}
  E\,r_E &= \bigl[E+(1-\tau)D\bigr]r_U+\tau D\,r_D-D\,r_D \nonumber\\
         &= E\,r_U+(1-\tau)D\,r_U-(1-\tau)D\,r_D \nonumber\\
  r_E &= r_U+(1-\tau)\frac{D}{E}(r_U-r_D). \label{eq:17rE}
\end{align}
With $D/E=0.95/0.05=19$ and $D/E=0.80/0.20=4$:
\begin{align}
  e=5\%:&\quad r_E=6\%+(0.80)(19)(2\%)=6\%+30.4\%=36.4\%, \nonumber\\
  e=20\%:&\quad r_E=6\%+(0.80)(4)(2\%)=6\%+6.4\%=12.4\%. \nonumber
\end{align}
Check with the after-tax WACC formula (slide 16):
\begin{align}
  e=5\%:&\quad (0.05)(36.4\%)+(0.95)(4\%)(0.80)=1.82\%+3.04\%=4.86\%, \nonumber\\
  e=20\%:&\quad (0.20)(12.4\%)+(0.80)(4\%)(0.80)=2.48\%+2.56\%=5.04\%. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(c)} After-tax WACC $4.86\%\to5.04\%$ (it gives back $V_L$); $r_E$ $36.4\%\to12.4\%$; both
checks hold.

\step{8}{(d) The government's claim} Discount $\tau X$ at $r_U$ and $\tau r_D D$ at $r_D$:
\begin{align}
  G &= \frac{\tau X}{r_U}-\frac{\tau r_D D}{r_D}=\frac{(0.20)(7.5)}{0.06}-\tau D=25-\tau D, \label{eq:17G}\\
  e=5\%:&\quad G=25-23.4568=1.5432,\qquad V_L+G=123.4568+1.5432=125, \nonumber\\
  e=20\%:&\quad G=25-19.0476=5.9524,\qquad V_L+G=119.0476+5.9524=125. \nonumber
\end{align}
The total $125=X/r_U=7.5/0.06$ is the pre-tax value of the assets, independent of $D$. The government
gains $5.9524-1.5432=4.4092$, exactly the shareholders' loss in \eqref{eq:17loss}.
\answer{(d)} $G=1.543$ at $5\%$ and $5.952$ at $20\%$; $V_L+G=125$ at both; the taxpayers gain the
$4.409$ the shareholders lose.

\step{9}{(e) Statement 3} For the bank's shareholders the statement is true: they lose the subsidy
$4.409$ and the after-tax WACC rises. For society it is a transfer, not a cost: the total value of the
claims on the assets stays at $125$, and the government's extra revenue can fund spending or lower
taxes elsewhere. The lost tax shield shows that the tax code subsidises bank debt, not that equity
uses up resources.
\answer{(e)} True privately, false socially: the lost tax shield is a transfer from bank shareholders to
taxpayers, so it does not make equity socially expensive.

\intuition Taxes add a third claimant to the bank. Debt lowers what the government takes, and that is
the only reason it raises $V_L$. A capital requirement moves that slice back to the government. Whether
banks should get the subsidy is a separate tax-policy question; it is no reason to keep leverage high.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Treating $e$ as $E/V_U$ instead of $E/V_L$, or computing $D=(1-e)V_U$; the requirement is a
        fixed point in $V_L$ \eqref{eq:17VL}.
  \item Counting the lost tax shield as a loss to society; it reappears in $G$.
  \item Holding $r_E$ at $36.4\%$ when equity rises (the Statement 2 mistake, again).
\end{itemize}

\section{The failure externality and the socially optimal capital ratio --- Original exercise}

\noindent\textit{Original exercise (list, Exercise 18), built on Topic 2B slides 44--47. All amounts
per \$1 of bank assets per year.}

\asked
(a) How likely failure is, and how much it costs society in expectation, at a thin and a thick capital ratio.
(b) Society's cost of the funding mix, why only two terms belong in it, and the ratio that minimises it.
(c) What the bank chooses on its own, and how its private cost of more capital compares with society's gain.
(d) How the slide-47 fallacies (Statements 2 and 3) bias a planner's ``optimal'' ratio downward.
(e) How $e^*$ responds to the social loss of a failure, the value of liquidity and asset risk.

\approach
This is the \emph{static trade-off} of slides 44--45 seen from society's side: the marginal social
benefit of debt (liquidity services) is set equal to the marginal expected cost of failure. By
Exercises 16--17 (MM without and with taxes), neither the equity premium nor the tax shield is a
social cost, so they are left out. The failure cost is a \emph{negative externality} (slide 47): the
bank does not bear it, which is why the private and social optima differ.

\notation
\begin{itemize}
  \item $e\in[0,1]$: equity ratio; $1-e$: deposits per \$1 of assets; $\ell\ge0$: annual loss rate, $\Pr(\ell>x)=\exp(-x/\theta)$, $\theta=0.01$.
  \item $p(e)=\Pr(\ell>e)$: annual failure probability; $\kappa=0.50$: social loss of a failure per \$1 of assets; $F(e)=\kappa p(e)$.
  \item $\lambda=0.005$: liquidity value per \$1 of deposits per year; $s=0.008$: tax saving per \$1 of deposits per year.
  \item $S(e)$: social cost of the funding mix; $e^*$: its minimiser; $\underline e$: regulatory minimum.
\end{itemize}

\step{1}{(a) Failure probability and expected failure cost} The bank fails when $\ell>e$, so
$p(e)=\exp(-e/\theta)$:
\begin{align}
  e=4\%:&\quad p=\exp(-0.04/0.01)=\exp(-4)=0.018316, \label{eq:18F}\\
        &\quad F=(0.50)(0.018316)=0.0091578, \nonumber\\
  e=20\%:&\quad p=\exp(-0.20/0.01)=\exp(-20)=2.06\times10^{-9}, \nonumber\\
        &\quad F=(0.50)(2.06\times10^{-9})=1.03\times10^{-9}. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(a)} $p(4\%)=1.83\%$ a year and $F(4\%)=0.916\%$ of assets a year; at $20\%$ failure is
practically impossible ($p=2.06\times10^{-9}$, $F=1.03\times10^{-9}$).

\step{2}{(b) The social cost function} Deposits yield a social benefit $\lambda(1-e)=\lambda-\lambda e$
a year, so relative to $e=0$ each unit of equity forgoes $\lambda$ of liquidity services. Adding the
expected failure cost and dropping the constant $-\lambda$:
\begin{align}
  S(e)=\lambda e+\kappa\exp(-e/\theta). \label{eq:18S}
\end{align}
The equity premium is absent because, by MM (Exercise 16), it only compensates shareholders for the
risk they bear and the total required return on the assets does not change. The tax saving $s$ is
absent because it is a transfer from the government (Exercise 17): what the bank gains, taxpayers lose.

\step{3}{(b) First- and second-order conditions} Differentiate \eqref{eq:18S}, using
$\frac{d}{de}\exp(-e/\theta)=-\frac1\theta\exp(-e/\theta)$:
\begin{align}
  S'(e) &= \lambda-\frac{\kappa}{\theta}\exp(-e/\theta)=0, \label{eq:18foc}\\
  S''(e) &= \frac{\kappa}{\theta^2}\exp(-e/\theta)>0, \nonumber
\end{align}
so the stationary point is the global minimum. Solve \eqref{eq:18foc}:
\begin{align}
  \exp(-e^*/\theta) &= \frac{\lambda\theta}{\kappa} \nonumber\\
  -\frac{e^*}{\theta} &= \ln\frac{\lambda\theta}{\kappa} \nonumber\\
  e^* &= \theta\ln\frac{\kappa}{\lambda\theta}. \label{eq:18estar}
\end{align}
$e^*>0$ because $\kappa/(\lambda\theta)>1$. Numerically,
\begin{align}
  \frac{\kappa}{\lambda\theta}=\frac{0.50}{(0.005)(0.01)}=\frac{0.50}{0.00005}=10{,}000,\qquad
  e^*=(0.01)\ln(10{,}000)=(0.01)(9.2103)=0.092103. \nonumber
\end{align}
By \eqref{eq:18estar} the failure probability at the optimum is
$p(e^*)=\lambda\theta/\kappa=0.00005/0.50=0.0001$, once in $10{,}000$ years.

\step{4}{(b) Social cost at three ratios}
\begin{align}
  S(4\%) &= (0.005)(0.04)+0.0091578=0.0002+0.0091578=0.0093578, \nonumber\\
  S(e^*) &= (0.005)(0.092103)+(0.50)(0.0001)=0.00046052+0.00005=0.00051052, \nonumber\\
  S(20\%) &= (0.005)(0.20)+1.03\times10^{-9}=0.0010000. \nonumber
\end{align}
\answer{(b)} $e^*=\theta\ln\bigl(\kappa/(\lambda\theta)\bigr)=9.21\%$, with failure probability $0.01\%$ a
year; $S(4\%)=0.93578\%$, $S(e^*)=0.05105\%$, $S(20\%)=0.10000\%$ of assets a year.

\step{5}{(c) The bank's choice} The shareholders' private cost $P(e)=(\lambda+s)e=0.013\,e$ has
$P'(e)=0.013>0$ at every $e$: each extra unit of equity only costs them, and the failure cost it would
avoid falls on others. The bank picks the lowest ratio allowed: $e=0$ unregulated, $e=\underline e=4\%$
under the minimum. Moving from $4\%$ to $e^*$:
\begin{align}
  \text{private cost} &= (0.013)(0.092103-0.04)=(0.013)(0.052103)=0.00067734, \nonumber\\
  \text{social gain} &= S(4\%)-S(e^*)=0.0093578-0.00051052=0.0088473. \nonumber
\end{align}
Of the private cost, $(0.005)(0.052103)=0.00026052$ is the liquidity loss (already inside $S$) and
$(0.008)(0.052103)=0.00041683$ is tax paid to the government.
\answer{(c)} The bank chooses $e=0$ unregulated and exactly $4\%$ under the minimum. The move to $e^*$
costs shareholders $0.0677\%$ of assets a year and saves society $0.8847\%$, about 13 times more.

\step{6}{(d) The planner's mistakes} Each mistake replaces $\lambda$ in \eqref{eq:18estar}:
\begin{align}
  \text{(i)}\quad \lambda+s=0.013:&\quad e=(0.01)\ln\frac{0.50}{(0.013)(0.01)}=(0.01)\ln(3{,}846.15)=(0.01)(8.2548)=0.082548, \nonumber\\
  \text{(ii)}\quad 0.10:&\quad e=(0.01)\ln\frac{0.50}{(0.10)(0.01)}=(0.01)\ln(500)=(0.01)(6.2146)=0.062146. \nonumber
\end{align}
The failure probabilities rise to $(0.013)(0.01)/0.50=0.00026$ and $(0.10)(0.01)/0.50=0.002$, and the
true social costs to $S=(0.005)(0.082548)+(0.50)(0.00026)=0.00041274+0.00013=0.00054274$ and
$S=(0.005)(0.062146)+(0.50)(0.002)=0.00031073+0.001=0.00131073$, both above $S(e^*)=0.00051052$.
\answer{(d)} (i) $8.25\%$, (ii) $6.21\%$, both below $e^*=9.21\%$; the larger the cost wrongly assigned
to equity, the thinner the ``optimal'' capital and the higher the true social cost.

\step{7}{(e) Comparative statics} Differentiate \eqref{eq:18estar}, written as
$e^*=\theta\bigl[\ln\kappa-\ln\lambda-\ln\theta\bigr]$:
\begin{align}
  \frac{\partial e^*}{\partial\kappa} &= \frac{\theta}{\kappa}=\frac{0.01}{0.50}=0.02>0, \nonumber\\
  \frac{\partial e^*}{\partial\lambda} &= -\frac{\theta}{\lambda}=-\frac{0.01}{0.005}=-2<0, \nonumber\\
  \frac{\partial e^*}{\partial\theta} &= \bigl[\ln\kappa-\ln\lambda-\ln\theta\bigr]+\theta\Bigl(-\frac1\theta\Bigr)=\ln\frac{\kappa}{\lambda\theta}-1=9.2103-1=8.2103>0. \nonumber
\end{align}
Doubling $\kappa$ changes $e^*$ by
\begin{align}
  \theta\ln\frac{2\kappa}{\lambda\theta}-\theta\ln\frac{\kappa}{\lambda\theta}=\theta\ln2=(0.01)(0.6931)=0.0069315, \nonumber
\end{align}
from $9.21\%$ to $(0.01)\ln(20{,}000)=9.90\%$.
\answer{(e)} $e^*$ rises with $\kappa$ ($+0.02$ per unit) and with $\theta$ ($+8.21$ per unit, since
$\kappa/(\lambda\theta)>e$), and falls with $\lambda$ ($-2$ per unit). Doubling $\kappa$ adds $0.69$
percentage points.

\intuition The bank's leverage choice is a trade-off in which it bears all of the benefit of debt and
none of the cost of failure. Society's trade-off counts both, and once the MM ``costs'' of equity are
seen to be risk compensation and transfers, the only real cost of equity left is the small loss of
liquidity services. That is why the social optimum is well above what banks would choose, and why
getting Statements 2 and 3 wrong biases capital requirements downward.

\pitfalls
\begin{itemize}
  \item Putting the equity premium or the tax shield into the social cost; both drop out by MM.
  \item Stating the first-order condition without differentiating $\exp(-e/\theta)$ (the factor
        $-1/\theta$ is where $\theta$ enters $e^*$).
  \item Reading the small private cost in (c) as proof that capital is cheap for society, or the
        reverse: the private cost includes a transfer, the social gain is the externality avoided.
\end{itemize}
