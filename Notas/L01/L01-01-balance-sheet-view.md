# 1.1–1.2 · The balance sheet as a split of one cash flow into promises

> **Claim.** A firm sells two things: goods and services, and **promises** on the cash those sales
> generate. Creditors hold senior promises, owners hold the junior residual. Because the two claims
> **exhaust** the asset's cash flow, their values add up to the asset's value. That is why
> $A=L+E$ holds in **market** value, and not only as a bookkeeping rule. Accountants measure the same
> identity at historical cost, which is where book and market part ways (note 03).

Prev: — · Next: [[L01-02-balance-sheet-items]] · Map: [[L01-00-map]]

**Notation.**

| Symbol | Meaning | Range |
|---|---|---|
| $A$, $L$, $E$ | assets, liabilities, shareholders' equity (book or market, stated each time) | $A,L\ge0$; book $E$ can be $<0$ |
| $X_t$ | cash flow generated by the assets at date $t$ (state-contingent) | real |
| $F$ | amount promised to creditors at a date | $F\ge0$ |
| $FCF_{A,t},FCF_{L,t},FCF_{E,t}$ | cash flow of the assets, paid to creditors, paid to owners | real |
| $\mathrm{PV}(\cdot)$ | the market's price today of a stream of cash flows | linear (§5) |

## 3 · C — the identity and why every transaction preserves it (slides 7–9)

Assets, on the left, summarize **how the firm uses its capital**; liabilities plus equity, on the
right, summarize **its sources** (slide 7). The slide-8 picture:

| Assets $A$ | Liabilities $L$ |
|---|---|
| "Resources that generate current and future cash flows for the corporation" | "Promises made to third parties" |
| | **Equity $E$** — "Resources not promised away to third parties" |

$$A=L+E \tag{1.1}$$

This is Berk's Balance Sheet Identity (2.1), p. 26. In the books it holds **by construction**: book
equity is *defined* as the difference $A-L$ (slide 22), so (1.1) cannot fail.

**Every transaction preserves it.** Take differences of (1.1) between two dates:

$$\Delta A=\Delta L+\Delta E \tag{1.2}$$

The slide-9 transaction, then three more for contrast (illustrations; the exercise on this is
Lista R1 Ex. 1, Higgins 1.4, p. 33):

| Transaction | $\Delta A$ | $\Delta L$ | $\Delta E$ | Check of (1.2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buy a 1 bn plant on credit (slide 9) | $+1$ | $+1$ | $0$ | $1=1+0$ |
| Repay 1 bn of debt with cash | $-1$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $-1=-1+0$ |
| Issue 1 bn of new shares for cash | $+1$ | $0$ | $+1$ | $1=0+1$ |
| Book 0.1 bn of depreciation (no tax) | $-0.1$ | $0$ | $-0.1$ (via net income) | $-0.1=0-0.1$ |

> **Only the last row touches the income statement.** The first three are pure balance-sheet moves;
> they change what the firm owns and owes but not what it earned. Depreciation reaches equity
> through net income, which is the bridge of note 04 §5.

## 4 · C — what gets recognized, and at what value (slides 9–10)

**Recognition.** The more tangible the asset, the easier it is to value and the likelier it is to be
recognized: cash, buildings, receivables. Patents, customer data, algorithms and brand reputation
are measured "more conservatively", often not at all, yet some estimates put intangibles at as much
as 90% of the S&P 500's total enterprise value (slide 9; the trend is slide 26, note 03 §4).

**Measurement.** Two bases (slide 10):

| Basis | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition (historical) cost | the original price paid for the asset, or received for the obligation | a plant at cost less accumulated depreciation (note 02 §2) |
| Market value | the current market price of the asset or liability | securities that are marked to market |

Silicon Valley Bank is the slide's recent example. Securities classified as **held to maturity** are
carried at (amortized) historical cost; securities **available for sale** are carried at market value.
When rates rise, the market value of a bond portfolio falls. The HTM part of the fall does not reach
the balance sheet, so book equity overstates what the assets would fetch.

Higgins (pp. 22–23) gives the accountants' reason. Faced with "relevant but subjective current
values" and "irrelevant but objective historical costs", they choose the objective numbers:
"Accountants prefer to be precisely wrong than approximately right." Fair-value accounting
marks some actively traded assets and liabilities to market, at the cost of more volatile
reported equity.

## 5 · C — two kinds of promises (slides 11–12)

A firm sells (i) goods and services, using PP&E, working capital and workers, whose sales produce the
cash flows behind the **market** value of its assets. It also sells (ii) **promises**: to fund the
assets it promises outside investors part of those future cash flows (slide 11).

| Promise | Holder | Rank | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liabilities | non-owners (creditors) | **senior** | bonds, bank loans, payables |
| Equity | owners (shareholders) | **junior, residual** | stock |

Owners are residual claimants: they get whatever is **not promised away** (slide 12). With one
date, a cash flow $X\ge0$ and a promise $F$ to creditors under limited liability:

$$\text{creditors receive } \min(X,F),\qquad \text{owners receive } \max(X-F,0) \tag{1.3}$$

**The two payoffs exhaust $X$ in every state.** Case $X\ge F$:

$$\min(X,F)+\max(X-F,0)=F+(X-F)=X$$

Case $X<F$:

$$\min(X,F)+\max(X-F,0)=X+0=X$$

So in every state

$$\min(X,F)+\max(X-F,0)=X \tag{1.4}$$

> **The choice between promises is "a key question in corporate finance" (slide 12).** Equation (1.4)
> already says the choice changes **who** gets the cash in each state, not **how much** cash there is.
> Whether it can change value is the Modigliani–Miller question of Topic 2A; the payoffs (1.3) come
> back there as equity being a call on the assets
> ([[../L02/L02-00-map|L02]], block 2A.5).

## 6 · C — why $A=L+E$ holds in market value (slide 13)

Slide 13: "the value of an asset is the expected discounted value of its future payments", and

$$FCF_A=FCF_L+FCF_E\ \Longrightarrow\ A=L+E \tag{1.5}$$

**Derivation.** Write the cash-flow split, date by date and state by state, as in (1.4):

$$FCF_{A,t}=FCF_{L,t}+FCF_{E,t}\quad\text{for every }t\text{ and every state} \tag{1.6}$$

Let $\mathrm{PV}(\cdot)$ be the market's price of a stream. In a market without arbitrage the price of
a sum of streams is the sum of their prices (the law of one price: a portfolio of the two claims
*is* the asset, so it must cost the same):

$$\mathrm{PV}(Y+Z)=\mathrm{PV}(Y)+\mathrm{PV}(Z) \tag{1.7}$$

Apply $\mathrm{PV}$ to both sides of (1.6):

$$\mathrm{PV}(FCF_A)=\mathrm{PV}(FCF_L+FCF_E)$$

Use (1.7) on the right-hand side:

$$\mathrm{PV}(FCF_A)=\mathrm{PV}(FCF_L)+\mathrm{PV}(FCF_E)$$

Name each price: $A^{\text{mkt}}=\mathrm{PV}(FCF_A)$, $L^{\text{mkt}}=\mathrm{PV}(FCF_L)$,
$E^{\text{mkt}}=\mathrm{PV}(FCF_E)$:

$$\boxed{\;A^{\text{mkt}}=L^{\text{mkt}}+E^{\text{mkt}}\;} \tag{1.8}$$

> **Read it.** The assumption doing the work is (1.7), **linear pricing**. Nothing about the split's
> shape matters: any $F$ in (1.3), any seniority, any number of claims. Two caveats come later.
> (i) If the split changes the cash flow itself (taxes, distress costs, incentives), (1.6) no longer
> holds with a fixed $FCF_A$. Taxes are the first case: the government is a third claimant, and
> note 06 §7 shows the corrected identity, (6.13). (ii) Each claim is discounted at its own risk-adjusted
> rate, and (1.8) then forces the asset's rate to be the value-weighted average of the claims' rates.
> That is the unlevering formula of [[../L02/L02-00-map|L02]], block 2A.2.

**Worked check with accounting flows (Ross, Brasil S/A, pp. 38–41).** Ross's "identidade do fluxo
de caixa" [2.3] (p. 37) is (1.6) measured over one year:

| Flow (2009) | Computation | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Operating cash flow | EBIT + depreciation − taxes $=694+65-212$ | 547 |
| Net capital spending | ending − beginning net fixed assets + depreciation $=1{,}709-1{,}644+65$ | 130 |
| Change in NWC | $1{,}014-684$ | 330 |
| **Cash flow from assets** | $547-130-330$ | **87** |
| Cash flow to creditors | interest − net new borrowing $=70-46$ | 24 |
| Cash flow to shareholders | dividends − new equity $=103-40$ | 63 |
| **Sum of the claims** | $24+63$ | **87** |

> **The identity checks: $87=24+63$.** Ross's operating cash flow subtracts **actual** taxes, so his
> cash flow from assets already contains the tax saved by deducting interest. The slides' unlevered
> FCF (slide 60) does not, and the two differ by exactly $\tau\times$interest. Note 06 §7 reconciles
> them on these same numbers.

## 7 · U — the cash flow–production cycle (slide 14)

Slide 14 reproduces Higgins's Figure 1.1 (13e, p. 4):

```
          Δ equity   taxes   Δ liabilities
                 \     |     /
  investment  <--  [ CASH ]  <--  collection of receivables
      |               |  ^                    ^
      v          production  cash sales        |
 [FIXED ASSETS]       v  |              [ACCOUNTS RECEIVABLE]
      | depreciation  [INVENTORY] -- credit sales --^
      +-------------->
```

Cash becomes inventory through production. Inventory becomes cash directly, through cash sales,
or later, through credit sales and the collection of receivables. That loop is the **working-capital
cycle**. Investment turns cash into fixed assets, and depreciation passes "a small portion of the value
of fixed assets" into each unit of inventory (Higgins pp. 4–5). Higgins draws two principles from
the figure (p. 5):

1. **Financial statements are a window on operations.** Credit terms, production technique and
   inventory control set the firm's financial profile. Tighter collection shows up as lower
   receivables.
2. **Profits do not equal cash flow.** A firm that lets customers pay ever later, or keeps producing
   more than it sells, can be profitable to the accountant and still run out of cash. This is the
   subject of note 06.
