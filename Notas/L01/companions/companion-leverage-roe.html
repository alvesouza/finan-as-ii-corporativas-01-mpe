Leverage and ROE

Two firms with the same operations and different financing. Return on invested capital measures the operations and ignores the financing. Return on equity adds leverage times a spread: ROIC minus the after-tax cost of debt. Move the debt share and the interest rate and watch ROE rise or fall along a straight line in D/E, while ROIC stays where it is.

Topic 1 slide 50 (Higgins two-firm example, τ = 40%); Higgins 13e Ch. 2 “Return on Invested Capital”, printed pp. 55–56 (same firms, τ = 25%); identity derived in Notas/L01/L01-05, eq. (5.20)

EBIT · operating profit

Earnings before interest and taxes: the operations, before the pie is split among lenders, the state and owners.

D+E · invested capital

Interest-bearing debt plus book equity. In the slide’s example this is also total assets.

D/(D+E) · debt share

How the same capital is financed. 0.90 is Company A (900 of 1,000); 0 is Company B.

rD · interest rate

Interest paid per unit of debt. Its after-tax value rD(1−τ) against ROIC sets the sign of the leverage effect.

τ · tax rate

Proportional tax on earnings before tax. Losses earn a refund at the same rate (full loss offset), which keeps the identity exact.

Expressions: D = w(D+E), E = (1−w)(D+E); interest = rDD; EBT = EBIT − interest; tax = τ·EBT; NI = EBT − tax; ROIC = EBIT(1−τ)/(D+E); ROE = NI/E; ROA = NI/(D+E) (assets = invested capital, as in slide 50); identity ROE = ROIC + (D/E)[ROIC − rD(1−τ)] (L01-05, eq. 5.20). Defaults reproduce slide 50’s Company A: ROE 18%, ROA 1.8%, ROIC 7.2%. “Company B” gives 7.2% three times. “Flat” sets rD = 12%, where rD(1−τ) = 7.2% = ROIC and leverage leaves ROE at 7.2%. “Higgins 13e” sets τ = 25%: ROE 22.5%, ROA 2.3% (2.25%), ROIC 9.0% (pp. 55–56). “Negative spread” sets rD = 16%: after-tax 9.6% > 7.2%, ROE −14.4%. With EBT < 0 the tax line is a refund; without full loss offset the identity fails there. The page is an accounting identity, not a valuation: whether the higher ROE is worth having is MM’s question (L02).
