       A would-be borrower is said to be rationed if he cannot obtain the
       loan that he wants even though he is willing to pay the interest that
       the lenders are asking, perhaps even a higher interest rate. In

       borrowers are constrained by ﬁxed lines of credit which they must not
       exceed in any circumstances, others are refused loans altogether.

Credit rationing

       Baltensperger (1978): credit rationing occurs when

       (...) some borrower’s demand for credit is turned down, even if this
       borrower is willing to pay all the price and nonprice elements of the
       loan contract.

       Priced element: interest rate; nonpriced element: collateral
       requirement, for example

       Note that a situation in which a borrower is turned down because
       he/she does not have enough collateral is not classiﬁed as credit
       rationing

       Similarly, the exclusion of some categories from credit markets
       (red-lining) because of a lack of future cash ﬂows or collateral is not
       credit rationing

Project: private beneﬁts

       aL entails a non-pecuniary private beneﬁt to the entrepreneur,
      which is equivalent to B > 0 dollars

       Two equivalent interpretations of B:

              Effort interpretation: effort is costly in terms of utility (working less
              and having more time for leisure is preferred to working hard).
              Therefore, B can be interpreted as the disutility of effort that is saved
              by shirking. Under this interpretation we use the notation aH = eH (high
              effort) and aL = eL (low effort, shirking)

              Project choice interpretation: the entrepreneur chooses a project
              that yields a high probability of success, or one that yields a smaller
              probability but that he likes more because it is easier to implement, is
              more prestigious, delivers perks, is more fun, is easier to commit fraud
              and embezzlement (e.g. buying inputs at an inﬂated price from a friend
              or family)

Credit rationing

      A borrower with wealth A < A does not obtain credit

      A smaller A means that the amount borrowed, I − A , is larger

       Lenders must break even, so lending a larger amount means that a
       larger fraction of the return R must be pledged to them

       Lenders simply turn down entrepreneurs with low A . An increase in
       the interest rate only exacerbates the moral hazard problem (smaller
       payoff in case of success, more incentives to choosing aL ).

Debt versus equity

       To ﬁnance the purchase of ﬁxed assets and inputs (raw materials,
       wages) that enable the sale of services and products, ﬁrms can issue

              Liabilities or promises to non-owners (creditors): speciﬁc and
              senior (e.g. bonds, bank loans, payables)

              Equity or promises to owners (shareholders): non-speciﬁc and
              junior (e.g. stocks)

       Owners are residual claimants (their claim is junior to liabilities): they
       receive whatever future cash ﬂows that are not promised away to
       non-owners (creditors)

       In other words, creditors have priority (their claim is senior to equity),
       and shareholders only get paid after fulﬁlling the promises to creditors

Optimality of the debt contract: limitations

       Debt is not a panacea
       Modigliani-Miller: the ﬁnancing method is irrelevant
       Optimality arises in this speciﬁc moral hazard setting with the
       assumption of risk-neutrality: high-powered incentives are optimal
       because there is no need for risk-sharing (and consumption
       smoothing)
       If the entrepreneur is risk-averse, the entrepreneur bears too much
       risk with a debt contract

                                                                                                                                                                                 66 / 69
Optimality of the debt contract: limitations

       Leverage also increases the risk of bankruptcy or ﬁnancial distress

       Financial distress and bankruptcy have costs that drain resources
       from the ﬁrm and distort managerial decisions

       Direct bankruptcy costs: legal and administrative costs, time and
       resources spent by managers and creditors dealing with bankruptcy,
       ﬁre sales of assets due to asset speciﬁcity and illiquid assets

       Indirect bankruptcy costs: loss of customers, key employees, and
       access to credit markets, conﬂict of interest between equityholders
       and creditors might lead to poor investment decisions (debt overhang,
       asset substitution)
