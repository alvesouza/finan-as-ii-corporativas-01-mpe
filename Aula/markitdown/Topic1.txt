The balance sheet

       A balance sheet is a snapshot of the ﬁrm’s ﬁnancial position taken at
       a given point in time
       The ﬁrm’s ﬁnancial position includes all the assets the ﬁrm owns and
       all the claims against those assets
       It is divided into two sides: assets (A ) on the left side and liabilities
       (L ) and shareholders’ equity (E) on the right
       Assets (A ) summarize how the ﬁrm uses its capital, and the right
      side (L + E) summarizes the sources of capital

The balance sheet view of the corporation

       Corporations sell two things
       (i) Goods and services

              Firms invest in assets (property, plant, and equipment or PP&E,
              working capital, etc.) and hire workers that enable those sales
              The sales generate current and future cash ﬂows that underpin the
              market value of its assets

       (ii) Promises
              Firms must raise money to invest in their assets
              They are able to raise money by promising to outside investors part of
              the future cash ﬂows generated from their sales

Promises

       There are two types of promises
       Liabilities or promises to non-owners (creditors): senior (e.g.
       bonds, bank loans, payables)
       Equity or promises to owners (shareholders): junior (e.g. stocks)
       Owners are residual claimants: they receive future cash ﬂows that
       are not promised away to non-owners (creditors)
       The choice between different types of promises is a key question in
       corporate ﬁnance

Financial statements: a quick review

       We will closely follow Berk and DeMarzo (2011)

       Current assets: assets that can be converted into cash within one
       year (liquid assets)
       Examples:

              Cash and other marketable securities: short-term, liquid, and low-risk
              investments (money market investments, government debt, bank
              deposits)
              Accounts receivable: amount owed by customers who have purchased
              goods and services on credit
              Inventories: raw materials, work-in-progress, and ﬁnished goods

                                                                                                                                                                                 15 / 63
Assets

       Non-current assets: “long-term” assets. Examples:
              Fixed assets such as property, plant, and equipment
              Goodwill: the difference between the market and the fair value (the
              accountant’s estimate of the potential value of an asset, not
              necessarily equal to book value) of an acquisition

       Over time, ﬁxed assets are worn out. The accountant reduces the
       book value of ﬁxed assets by an amount known as depreciation

       Depreciation is not a cash expense

       The book value of an asset: acquisition cost minus accumulated
       depreciation

Shareholders’ equity

       Difference between assets and liabilities: book value of equity

       Accounting measure of the net worth of the ﬁrm

       The book value of equity differs from the actual amount that investors
       are willing to pay for the equity portion of the ﬁrm, known as the
       market value or market capitalization or market cap

       Market value of a publicly traded company: share price times the
       number of common shares outstanding

       Market values are forward-looking (they reﬂect the expected
       discounted value of future payoffs to shareholders), while book
       values are backward-looking

                                                                                                                                                                                 22 / 63
Shareholders’ equity

       Examples of drivers of the difference between book and market
       values

       The values of most of the assets on the balance sheet are computed
       using historical costs rather than their current market value

              Historical costs do not incorporate changes to the future income the
              assets might generate

              Only a few assets and liabilities are actively traded and can be marked
              to market

       Many of the ﬁrms’ valuable assets (or liabilities) do not appear on the
       balance sheet:

              Assets: quality of the employees and the management team, value of
              future R&D innovations, reputation, loyal customers; liabilities: pending
              lawsuits (e.g. regarding product liability claims)

Debt-equity ratio

       Debt-equity ratio: total liabilities divided by shareholders’ equity

       We can compute this measure using either book or market values for
       equity and debt

       It is a measure of the ﬁrm’s leverage

       The market debt-equity ratio is more informative about the ﬁrm’s
       leverage and capacity to meet future debt payments

              Even ﬁrms with negative book equity (recall the Starbucks example)
              might be able to contract a lot of debt because they are expected to be
              proﬁtable in the future

              Particularly useful for assessing the leverage of high-growth and
              start-up ﬁrms

Income statement

       The income statement or proﬁt and loss statement (P&L)
       attempts to measure the proﬁts the ﬁrm generates during a period of
       time

              It lists the ﬁrm’s revenues and expenses over a period of time

              Revenues increase the book value of equity, while expenses reduce it

       Accountants apply the accrual principle: revenue is recognized at
       the time of the sale, not when the customer pays. Similarly, expenses
       are recorded when goods and services are purchased, not when
       suppliers are paid

       The accrual method matches revenues and expenses in the period in
       which the transaction occurs (the matching principle)

Income statement

       The accountant does not charge the full cost of a long-term ﬁxed
       asset in the year of the investment

              The proﬁt would be too low in the year of the investment, and too high
              in the other productive years of the asset (as the asset contributes to
              revenues but not to expenses)

       The solution is to spread the cost of the asset over its productive life
       in the form of depreciation

       It is a way to properly match revenues and expenses during a period

       Depreciation is not a cash expense; the cash outlay only occurs at
       the time of the investment

                                                                                                                                                                                 34 / 63
Income statement

       The amount of depreciation depends on the length of the productive
       life of the asset, its salvage value, and the method of allocation
       (straight line, accelerated)
       Also following the matching principle, accountants do not record
       increases in inventories as an expense

Income statement

       Gross proﬁt: revenues from sales of products minus costs incurred
       to make and sell the products (costs of goods sold or cost of
       sales)

       Other operating expenses: expenses from the ordinary course of
       running the business that are not directly related to producing the
       goods being sold (that is, other than costs of goods sold). Examples:
       administrative expenses and overhead, salaries, marketing costs,
       R&D expenses, depreciation

       We can assume that most of the expenses in costs of goods sold are
       variable (change as sales vary), while most of the other operating
       expenses are ﬁxed (independent of the amount of sales)

              Companies with a high proportion of ﬁxed costs are more vulnerable to
              sale declines (high operating leverage)

                                                                                                                                                                                 36 / 63
Income statement

       Operating income: revenues net of operating expenses

       Earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT): operating income plus
       any net income related to activities that are not a core part of the
       ﬁrm’s business (nonoperating income)

              It is a measure of the ﬁrm’s income before it is split among
              debtholders, equityholders, and the tax authorities

       Net income or (net) earnings or (net) proﬁts or bottom line: EBIT
       minus interest expenses and taxes

              Apart from any equity issuance or payout to equityholders, net income
              determines the growth in book equity

       The statement of equity or statement of changes in equity reports the
       changes in the ﬁrm’s equity
      [ Beginning book equity + net income - dividends +/- other changes ]

ROE as a measure of performance

       Is a company with a high ROE better than a company with a smaller
       one? Does an increase in ROE mean the ﬁrm’s performance has
       improved?

       Not necessarily. ROE is backward-looking and focused on a single
       year

              It would fall after an equity issuance to fund a project because the
              investment takes a while to turn into proﬁts

       ROE ignores risk: ROE might be high simply because the business is
       very risky

       ROE measures the return over the book value of equity, not the
       market value (the current value of shares)

Statement of cash ﬂows

       The statement of cash ﬂows reports how much cash ﬂow a ﬁrm has
       generated during a given period and how that cash ﬂow has been
       allocated
       It measures how the ﬁrm’s cash balance changes over time
       Investors are ultimately interested in the amount of cash ﬂows they
       can extract from the business

Statement of cash ﬂows: operating activities

       The statement of cash ﬂows is divided into three sections
       Operating activities: it starts with net income and adds back all
       non-cash entries (e.g. depreciation, deferred taxes) related to the
       ﬁrm’s operating activities
       Depreciation: it is deducted when computing net income but it is not
       a cash expense. Therefore, we add it back to net income to measure
       the amount of cash the ﬁrm has generated
       Same with other non-cash expenses such as amortization and
       accrued items such as salaries and taxes

                                                                                                                                                                                 54 / 63
Statement of cash ﬂows: operating activities

       Accounts receivable: sales are recorded as part of net income even
       when the payment has not been received. We must adjust cash ﬂows
       by deducting the increases in accounts receivable
       Similarly, we add back increases in accounts payable: purchases
       from suppliers that have not been paid yet
       We deduct increases to inventory, as they are not recorded as an
       expense in the income statement

       Purchases of property, plant, and equipment (capital expenditures)
       are not recorded in the income statement
       Instead, the the ﬁrm depreciates (consumes) these assets and
       deducts depreciation over time
       We have to subtract capital expenditures

Measures of cash ﬂow

       Free cash ﬂow (FCF) to the ﬁrm or unlevered free cash ﬂow: total
       cash available for distribution to owners and creditors after funding all
       investment activities:

       FCF = EBIT(1 - Tax rate) + Depreciation - Capital expenditures -
       Working capital investments

              EBIT(1 - Tax rate): income the company earns regardless of how the
              business is ﬁnanced, that is, excluding interest expenses associated
              with debt ﬁnancing (unlevered or all-equity)

              Depreciation (and any other noncash items) are not cash outlays

              Working capital investment: increase in current assets necessary to
              support the ﬁrm’s operations, minus any increase in non-interest
              bearing current liabilities

Measures of cash ﬂow

       Free cash ﬂow to equity (FCFE) or levered free cash ﬂow: total
       cash available for distribution to equityholders or for reinvestment in
       the business after meeting interest payments

              Essentially, cash ﬂow from operating activities minus capital
              expenditures

              Or FCF minus (after tax) interest expenses

              We should also include net debt issuance (or deduct net debt
              repayment) if the ﬁrm is changing its level of debt

       Forecasts of FCF are used to compute the market enterprise value,
       while forecasts of FCFE are used to determine the market equity
       value
