The asset substitution problem

       Consider a ﬁrm that generates a random liquidating payoff of X
      tomorrow (at t = 1)

              We can think of X as the resale value of the ﬁrm

      The ﬁrm has debt with a face value of K at t = 1

      Debt is risky, meaning that with positive probability X ≤ K
      The equity payoff is max{X − K , 0}, the same payoff of a long
       position in a call option on the assets of the ﬁrm, X , with strike K

              The equityholder bears the upside (any quantity above K accrues to
              him/her), and obtains zero whenever losses are such that X < K ⇒
              preference for riskier projects

                                                                                                                                                                                   2 / 62
The asset substitution problem

      The debt payoff is min{X, K } = K − max{0, K − X} the same payoff of
       a long position in a risk-free bond with face value K and a short
       position in a put option on the assets of the ﬁrm, X , with strike K

              Debtholders have priority and they only care about the ﬁrst K dollars ⇒
              preference for safer projects

      Different payoffs ⇒ conﬂict of interest between equity and
       debtholders

       The preferred choices of debtholders and shareholders can differ and
       are not necessarily equal to the choice that maximizes ﬁrm value

       As equityholders have control rights, ﬁrms tend to pick riskier projects
       when leverage is high, and not necessarily the ones that maximize
       ﬁrm value (the asset substitution or risk-shifting problem)

Asset substitution: implications

       Firms whose business activities are such that managers (acting in the
       interest of shareholders) have more discretion with regard to risk
       choices should have less debt and prioritize equity ﬁnancing

       On the other hand, ﬁrms in which managers have little discretion with
       regard to risk can have more debt

              E.g. mature industries, regulated public utilities

       Risk-shifting distortions are higher when the ﬁrm is in ﬁnancial
       distress (gambling for resurrection)

              In particular, owners might delay ﬁling for bankruptcy so as to keep the
              option value of equity alive

Asset substitution: mitigation

       Our framework is static (one-period)

       In a multi-period setting the need to access markets in the future
       generates an incentive to build a good reputation and can mitigate the
       problem

       Jensen and Meckling (1976):

       It seems clear, for instance, that the expectation of future sales of
       outside equity and debt will change the costs and beneﬁts facing the
       manager in making decisions which beneﬁt himself at the (short–run)
       expense of the current bondholders and stockholders. If he develops
       a reputation for such dealings, he can expect this to unfavorably
       inﬂuence the terms at which he can obtain future capital from outside
       sources. This will tend to increase the beneﬁts associated with
       “sainthood” and will tend to reduce the size of the agency costs.

Asset substitution: mitigation

       Short-term debt: frequent repricing that incorporates any increases in
       risk and the threat to not be able to rollover the debt lessen incentives
       for excessive risk taking

       Covenants (provisions that restrict the actions that ﬁrms can take):
       e.g. prohibit investments in new, unrelated lines of business; indirect
       effects of strict covenants if safer projects enable ﬁrms to meet the
       required ratios (e.g. safer projects generate cash ﬂows earlier, as in
       Gilje, 2016)

       Convertible debt: debtholders can exercise the option to become
       equityholders if the business booms

              Equityholders are no longer the sole residual claimants in case of
              success: part of the gains accrue to debtholders

Asset substitution: friction

       What is the friction underlying the risk-shifting problem?
       Commitment: managers (acting on behalf of shareholders) cannot
       commit to taking good investments once debt is contracted
       Similar to the Holmstrom and Tirole (1997) model, shareholders pay
       the cost by not receiving funding or paying a higher interest rate
       If equity is costly/difﬁcult to raise, ﬁrms might forgo proﬁtable
       investment opportunities
       Impossible to write contracts on project choices to guarantee such a
       commitment

Debt overhang

       After debt is contracted, proﬁtable investments connected to the
       assets in place of a ﬁrm might not be undertaken
       The reason is that borrowers (existing shareholders) bear all the
       investment costs (money, time, effort) but part of the returns accrue to
       existing creditors
       In other words, debt in place acts as an implicit tax on investment, the
       tax rate being related to the probability of default

                                                                                                                                                                                 19 / 62
Debt overhang (cont’d)

       The problem is more severe when default is more likely, as the portion
       of returns that accrue to lenders (the ‘tax burden’) increases
       The problem is less severe if the value of the relationship with the
       original creditors is large or if default costs are increasing in the
       amount owed (dynamic repayment incentives)
       Originally a conjecture about ﬁrms’ behavior (Myers, 1977), the notion
       of debt overhang has been extended (and documented) to individuals

       Borrowers with negative home equity (value of the house is smaller

              The efﬁciency loss is larger for investment opportunities whose value

Default as insurance

      When A + Y ≥ D (or Y ≥ D − A ), the individual defaults whenever
                               u(A + Y − D) < u(A + E) − σ

              Since the individual is risk-averse, default can increase welfare by
              making the individual bear less income risk

Identifying information asymmetries in credit markets

       In general, it is difﬁcult to disentangle adverse selection and moral
       hazard

       Consider the following experiment:
              Randomly allocate individuals into different groups
              Then offer the same loan contract at different prices (interest rates) to
              the groups of otherwise identical individuals

       A positive correlation between interest rates and default can arise due
       to

              Adverse selection: those with a relatively high probability of default are
              more likely to accept a high rate; or
              Moral hazard: a high interest rate can undermine incentives and
              increase the default probability

              Interest rate offered in a direct-mail solicitation: the offer rate

       The design enables the comparison of
              Borrowers who select in at different rates and then face identical

Model

Three stages:

       Stage 1: the individual decides whether or not to take up a loan with
       an exogenous interest rate ro. The rate ro does not convey any
       information about interest rates of future loans: the individuals believe
       that future loans will be offered at a normal rate r.

       Stage 2: The lender randomly lowers the interest rate for some
      borrowers to rc < r0. With an independent randomization, the lender
      lowers the interest rate of future loans to rf = rc < r for some
       borrowers (conditional on repaying the loan). Given rc and rf the
       borrower chooses how much effort to put into generating cash ﬂows
       to repay the current loan

       Stage 3: Cash ﬂows are realized and the borrower decides whether
       or not to repay the loan
