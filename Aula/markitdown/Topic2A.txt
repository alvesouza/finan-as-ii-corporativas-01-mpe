Project adoption rules: NPV

       We have built a framework to value (price) assets and projects
       When should we adopt a project?
       The net present value (NPV) project adoption rule states that a
       project should be undertaken if its NPV is positive
      For any asset (project) A that requires an investment of cA at t = 0,
       the NPV is deﬁned by

                                       NPVA = PVA − cA

                                                                                                                                                                               28 / 105
Project adoption rules: NPV

       PVA : value of the stream of cash ﬂows “inside the ﬁrm”

       cA : cost of the stream of cash ﬂows or value of the stream of cash
       ﬂows “outside the ﬁrm”

       A positive NPV project represents an “arbitrage” across the
       boundaries of the ﬁrm

              Unlike other hypothetical arbitrage opportunities, positive NPV projects
              are not scalable, so they can exist in equilibrium

       A positive NPV projects enrich (in expectation) the current claimants
       on the assets of the ﬁrm (equityholders and debtholders)

              How equityholders and debtholders split the positive NPV can distort
              decisions (e.g. debt overhang, later in the course)

                                                                                                                                                                               29 / 105
Project adoption rules: NPV

       An example: Suppose ﬁrm P (acquirer) is considering to buy out ﬁrm
       T (target), which is currently held by a single shareholder S

              If S is willing to sell the target company T for cT , whereas, based on
              P’s valuation, the value of ﬁrm T is PVT , then the acquisition is a
              positive NPV investment if PVT > cT
              Why does the valuation of the ﬁrm T differ when it is operated as a
              separate entity owned by S, cT , and when it forms a conglomerate with
              P?

              It could be that T being part of P increases efﬁciency (synergies,
              economies of scale/scope, better management practices) or even
              market power gains

Financing choices

       Capital structure is the composition of the claims against a ﬁrm’s
       assets
       The most common form of liability is debt
       Differences between equity and debt:
       (i) Payoff structure: debt contracts establish ﬁxed promised
       payments at ﬁxed dates; the payoff of equity contracts is variable in
       nature as equityholders are residual claimants

                                                                                                                                                                               39 / 105
Financing choices

       (ii) Seniority: Debt is senior to equity, that is, shareholders only get
       paid after fulﬁlling the promises to debtholders
       (iii) Maturity: Debt claims always have a ﬁxed maturity, whereas
       equity claims are perpetual
       (iii) Legal rights: debtholders do not have control rights, that is, they
       do not have a say in the management of the ﬁrm; equity holders, on
       the other hand, have voting rights

                                                                                                                                                                               40 / 105
Financing choices

       Even though debtholders do not have control rights, they can impose
       restrictive covenants to protect their interests
       Examples: covenants restricting dividend payments, share
       repurchases, new debt issuance (especially secured or senior to
       current debt); covenants that transfer control in case conditions such
       as maximum leverage, minimum net worth (book value of assets
       minus book value of liabilities) or minimum liquidity, are violated
       Violations of covenants (other than the repayment of principal and
       interest) are referred to as technical defaults

Digression: market completeness

       We say the market is complete when it is possible to construct
       contingent claims for every possible state of the world

              The contingent claim does not have to be traded explicitly
              Investors only need to be able to synthesize or replicate all contingent
              claims by combining (buying and short-selling) traded securities
       Law of One Price: assets and combination of assets (portfolios) that
       have the same payoff should trade at the same price
              Investors cannot make instantaneous proﬁts by repackaging portfolios
              (there is no arbitrage)

                                                                                                                                                                               50 / 105
Digression: market completeness

       An arbitrage exists

              If you can construct a portfolio that has a positive payoff today (inﬂow
              of cash) and does not require any payment back in the future, that is, a
              free lunch; or

              If you can create a portfolio that has zero costs today but has a
              nonnegative payoff in every possible future scenario (strictly positive
              with positive probability), that is, a money machine

       An arbitrage does not exist in equilibrium, provided there is at least
       one rational investor who prefers more to less (non-satiation)

       In particular, assets with the same future stream of payoffs should
       have the same price (law of one price)

Comment on rD

The expected return on debt rD is the discount rate that makes the
present value of expected payments equal to the debt’s market price:

               D=            F × (1 − p) + C × p
