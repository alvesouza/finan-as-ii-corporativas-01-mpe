Hidden information

       In many markets, on side has better information than the other

       Examples:

              Sellers of used cars know more about the quality of their cars than
              potential buyers

              Clients seeking for insurance know more than the insurance providers
              about their accident risk

              Workers know more than employers about their abilities

              Borrowers know more than lenders about their default probability, the
              value of assets in place and pledged collateral, and the private beneﬁt
              associated with different investment choices

              In an IPO some (connected) investors know more about the ﬁrm than
              others

A model of ﬁnancing under asymmetric information

       A penniless borrower (entrepreneur, ﬁrm) needs I to invest in a
       project that generates R in case of success and 0 in case of failure
      (A1) The borrower and the outside investors are risk-neutral ⇒ all
       future payoffs are discounted at the risk-free rate (risk premium = 0)
      (A2) The risk-free interest rate is normalized to zero: rf = 0
      (A1) + (A2) ⇒ discount rate for any future payoff is r = 0

                                     r = risk premium + rf = 0
       Capital markets are competitive and outside investors have access to
       a risk-free bond that yields a zero net return

Asymmetric information

       As lenders cannot distinguish between good and bad borrowers,
       there is only one equilibrium contract for both types that requires a
       repayment of Ru in case of success
      As Ru ≤ R because of limited liability, Rf = R − Ru ≥ 0 and both the

      In particular, the maximum proﬁt of outside investors is mR − I

Pecking-order hypothesis

       To minimize adverse selection costs, ﬁrms prefer to use internal funds
       to ﬁnance the projects

       To avoid being detected, the bad type would follow suit

       The reason is that debt is less information-sensitive than equity

       The reason is that the pool of borrowers gets worse once interest

       Kawai et al. (2022): We study how signaling affects equilibrium
       outcomes and welfare in an online credit market, using detailed data
       on loan characteristics and borrower repayment. We build and
       estimate an equilibrium model in which a borrower may signal her
       default risk through the reserve interest rate. Comparing markets with
       and without signaling relative to the benchmark with no asymmetric
       information, we ﬁnd that adverse selection destroys as much as 34%

       consistent with the prediction made by Stiglitz and Weiss in 1981.
